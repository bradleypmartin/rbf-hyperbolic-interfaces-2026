\begin{abstract}
Between a material whose parameters vary smoothly on the scale of the grid
and one with a jump lies the case of an edge that is smooth but changes over
a distance $\delta$ far smaller than the grid spacing $h$. Standard
high-order finite differences sample the coefficients at the nodes and lose
their order there until $h \lesssim \delta$, and the interface-aware
stencils built for a jump do not apply, since there is no jump. We build
stencils on the same equispaced (1-D) or scattered (2-D) nodes whose basis
functions are not monomials but \emph{seeds}: the $t = 0$ profiles of
solutions of the wave equation that are polynomial in time, continued
through the edge by ordinary differential equations in the coordinate normal
to it. In one dimension the seeds form an extended complete Chebyshev system
for every $\delta$, so the stencil solve is nonsingular, and as
$\delta \to 0$ they reduce to the translated polynomial basis of the
interface-aware stencils, so one construction covers the jump, the
sub-grid edge and the resolved edge. In one dimension, standard fourth-order finite differences have a
knee at $h = \delta$ while the seed stencils are fourth order at every
resolution with the errors of the jump case. In two dimensions, for the
elastic wave equation discretised with radial basis function-generated
finite differences (RBF-FD) on scattered nodes, the seeds of a straight
edge reduce to triangular systems of ordinary differential equations in the
normal coordinate; the seed-augmented operator keeps the standard scheme's stability at the
standard hyperviscosity (its rightmost eigenvalues at the level of the
jump-aware operator's, the full rank of the augmentation checked
numerically), cuts the error through an unresolved edge by up to an order
of magnitude or more on the node sets tested, and leads to the rule: seed
when $\delta \le h$. The same seeds marched along the true normal handle a
curved edge at the same accuracy on those node sets, up to a $1.5\times$
geometry penalty through the sharpest edge on the finest of them.
\end{abstract}

High-order finite differences for wave propagation are in good shape at
both ends of the range of media. When the material parameters vary
smoothly on the scale of the grid, sampling them at the nodes costs
nothing and a fourth-order stencil stays fourth order. When they jump,
the stencils that straddle the jump can be rebuilt from piecewise
polynomials that satisfy the interface conditions, and the scheme keeps
its order on a grid that takes no notice of the interface
\cite{Martin2016,MartinFornberg2017}. Between the two lies a case that is
neither. An edge can be smooth, so that no interface condition applies,
and still change over a distance $\delta$ smaller than the grid spacing
$h$. Any smooth model contains such edges once it is discretised coarsely
enough, and any jump becomes one under the slightest smoothing.

A standard stencil through such an edge samples the coefficients at its
nodes, sees a jump, and treats it as if the coefficients were smooth in
between, which is the first-order failure of the jump case. It recovers
only once $h \lesssim \delta$. In the one-dimensional experiment of
Section~\ref{sec:results1d}, fourth-order finite differences (FD4)
through an edge of width $\delta = 0.0025$ converge at rates $1.3$ and
$2.2$ while $h > \delta$, drop by a factor $200$ between $n = 400$ and
$n = 800$ nodes ($h = 2\delta$ to $h = \delta$), and fourth order on
average after that: a knee at $h = \delta$. The two existing repairs do not
reach this regime. The interface-aware stencils built for a jump are the
$\delta \to 0$ limit of the right construction, as
Section~\ref{sec:seeds1d} shows, but their weights are off at first order
in $\delta / h$, by $10\%$ of the largest weight at $\delta = h/10$ and
$1\%$ at $\delta = h/100$ in the example of Section~\ref{sec:jumplimit}.
The other route expands the coefficients in Taylor series about the
edge, as the interface-aware construction can for a wave speed that
varies smoothly up to an interface \cite{Martin2016,MartinFornberg2017}
and as polynomial quasi-Trefftz spaces do
in general \cite{ImbertGerardMoiolaStocker2023}; but a profile such as
$\tanh(x / \delta)$ has poles at $\pm i \pi \delta / 2$, so the expansion
converges only within $\pi \delta / 2$ of the edge while the stencil
reaches $2h \gg \delta$.

The construction of this paper keeps everything about the scheme except
the stencils that see the edge, which take the footprint the jump
construction gave them and a new basis. In place of the
monomials $1, x, x^2, \ldots$ a stencil is made exact on \emph{seeds}:
the $t = 0$ profiles of solutions of the wave equation that are
polynomial in time. For the operator $\Lu = \rho^{-1} \px K \px$ that
governs the particle velocity, such a profile satisfies $\Lu^m g = 0$
for some $m$, and with constant coefficients these profiles are exactly
the polynomials. Anchored at the stencil's evaluation point $x_e$ they
form a chain, $\phi_0 = 1$, $K \phi_1' = K(x_e)$,
$\Lu \phi_k = k (k - 1) c_e^2 \phi_{k-2}$ with $\phi_k(x_e) = \phi_k'(x_e)
= 0$, which is integrated through the edge as a first-order system of
ordinary differential equations that never differentiates the
coefficients: one short march per stencil that sees the edge. Three
facts make this a method rather than a trick. The seeds form an extended
complete Chebyshev system for every $\delta$, so the per-stencil solve is
nonsingular on any distinct nodes, including at $\delta = 0$ where the
seeds have kinks, with the conditioning of a Vandermonde matrix
(condition numbers $20$ to $60$ from $\delta = 10^{-5}$ to $1$). The
truncation error of the seed stencil on a solution is
$O(h^4\, \px \pt^4 u)$, the $x$-derivative of another solution in place
of FD4's $O(h^4 u^{(5)})$ with $u^{(5)} \sim \delta^{-4}$ inside the edge;
that the constant does not depend on $\delta$ is the prediction the
experiments test, not a theorem. And
the chain read in the weak sense across a jump is the translated Taylor
basis of the interface-aware stencils, so one construction covers the
jump (algebra), the sub-grid edge (an ODE march) and the resolved edge,
where the seeds and the monomials give different weights, first order in
$h / \delta$ apart, and both are fourth order on solutions.

The same idea carries to two dimensions, to the elastic wave equation
on scattered nodes discretised with radial basis function-generated
finite differences (RBF-FD). For a straight edge the material depends on
the normal coordinate only, and an ansatz polynomial in the tangential
coordinate turns the chain into triangular systems of one-dimensional
ODEs in the normal coordinate: all $30$ seeds of one stencil, the
monomials to degree four in each velocity component, march as one linear
system of $600$ states anchored at the evaluation node. The seeds
replace the polynomial augmentation of the interface-aware RBF-FD
stencils; the Gaussian part of the stencil and the coupled saddle-point
solve stay as they are. For a curved edge the same seeds are marched
along the true normal through the stencil's foot point, an approximation
of zeroth order in the curvature, which is the one the jump stencils
already make.

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item In one dimension: the seed chain, its nested kernels, its
  well-posedness as an extended complete Chebyshev system, and the jump
  construction as its $\delta \to 0$ limit (Section~\ref{sec:seeds1d}).
  On the same equispaced grid the seed stencils are fourth order at every
  resolution, with errors within $0.3\%$ of the jump case's through the
  sharpest edge tested, while FD4 has its knee at $h = \delta$
  (Section~\ref{sec:results1d}).
\item In two dimensions: the elastic seeds of a straight edge as
  triangular systems of ODEs in the normal coordinate, marched as one
  system per stencil; their assembly into seed rows for the elastic
  operator and hyperviscosity rows that annihilate the seeds; and the
  finding that the seeded operator keeps the standard scheme's stability
  at the standard hyperviscosity at every edge width, by the spectral
  and energy measurements of Section~\ref{sec:stability2d}
  (Sections~\ref{sec:seeds2d} and~\ref{sec:results2d}).
\item A rule: \emph{seed when $\delta \le h$}. Through an edge the nodes
  never resolve the seeds are fourth order at every resolution tested,
  $20\times$ below the standard scheme in the velocity and $67\times$ in
  the spurious tangential velocity at $19{,}600$ nodes, at the seed
  operator's own resolution floor; the crossover is at $h \approx
  \delta$; and through a resolved edge the seed stencils are $1.4$ to
  $2\times$ worse than the standard ones, so a resolved edge should not
  be seeded.
\item At oblique incidence the tangential dependence of the seeds is
  what acts: the full seeds beat the standard scheme by $1.4$ to
  $1.9\times$ and the normal-only ablation is worse than doing nothing
  through a sharp edge. Every scheme converges at between second and
  two-and-a-half order in the velocities there, the jump-aware stencils
  included, because the mode-converted shear waves sit at a
  pre-asymptotic floor on these node sets; the result is reported as it
  is.
\item Through a curved edge the jump stencils sit at the resolution
  floor at every node count, and the seeds marched along the true normal
  equal the flat seeds at their own floor through $\delta = 0.005$ and
  $0.01$, $2.2$ to $7\times$ below the standard scheme at $\delta = 0.005$,
  and are $3$ to $12\times$ below it at $\delta = 0.0025$ on node sets up
  to $19{,}600$ nodes, where the zeroth-order geometry shows as $1.4$ to
  $1.5\times$ the flat seeds' error on the two finest of them.
\item An open implementation in Python, written with the AI tools
  disclosed at the end of the paper, with a test for every numerical
  routine, in which every figure and table of this paper is regenerated
  by a committed script from a results cache
  (\url{https://github.com/bradleypmartin/20260930-zd-ai-pdes-demo/tree/manuscript-v1},
  the tag of this version).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Relation to prior work}
\label{sec:priorwork}
% Placed from LITERATURE.md §6a (#53), trimmed as that section allows: the
% Chebyshev-systems sentence is dropped (Section 3.3 cites them) and the two
% jump-method sentences are merged; the last three sentences are verbatim,
% they carry the obligations of LITERATURE.md §1a K6 and §1b O1. Any change
% to the novelty language re-opens the ledger first.
Three lines of prior work meet here, and the construction adds nothing to
any of them taken alone. Stencils and elements built from local solutions
of the operator are the fitted operators of the 1950s and 60s
\cite{AllenSouthwell1955,Ilin1969,ScharfetterGummel1969,RoosStynesTobiska2008}
and the exact schemes that followed \cite{Mickens1993,VizvariEtAl2020};
the constant-flux seed in particular is the harmonic mean of Tikhonov and
Samarskii \cite{TikhonovSamarskii1962,Samarskii2001}, the special elements
of Babu\v{s}ka and Osborn \cite{BabuskaOsborn1983,BabuskaCalozOsborn1994},
the multiscale elements of Hou and Wu \cite{HouWu1997} and the harmonic
coordinates of Owhadi and Zhang \cite{OwhadiZhang2007}, whose extension to
the acoustic wave equation \cite{OwhadiZhang2008} is the closest
two-dimensional relative of what follows. Basis functions that are
polynomial-in-time solutions of the wave equation are the Trefftz spaces
of space--time discontinuous Galerkin methods
\cite{KretzschmarMoiolaPerugiaSchnepp2016,BanjaiGeorgoulisLijoka2017,MoiolaPerugia2018};
their quasi-Trefftz extension to variable coefficients
\cite{ImbertGerardMoiolaStocker2023,ImbertGerardMoiolaPerinatiStocker2025,ImbertGerard2025local}
builds them by Taylor expansion at a point, the route
Section~\ref{sec:taylor} shows cannot reach an edge thinner than the
stencil. For a jump, the interface-aware stencils this work generalises
\cite{Martin2016,MartinFornbergStCyr2015,MartinFornberg2017} stand beside
the immersed interface \cite{LeVequeLi1994,ZhangLeVeque1997,SabatiniEtAl2023},
explicit simplified interface \cite{PirauxLombard2001,LombardPiraux2004}
and summation-by-parts \cite{MattssonNordstrom2006,DuruVirta2014,GranathWang2025}
treatments, all of which take the jump as given, where standard schemes
are first order \cite{Brown1984,SymesVdovina2009,VishnevskyEtAl2014}.
The standing alternative in seismic and electromagnetic finite differences
changes the medium rather than the stencil: coefficients averaged over a
cell \cite{Backus1962,MuirEtAl1992,MoczoEtAl2002,KristekEtAl2017,JiangZhang2024,FarjadpourEtAl2006},
thin structure homogenised
\cite{CapdevilleGuillotMarigo2010a,CapdevilleGuillotMarigo2010b}, or the
jump smoothed over a few cells
\cite{TornbergEngquist2003,TornbergEngquist2006,KoeneWittstenRobertsson2022}.
Those methods choose the smoothing width from the grid and accept a
second-order error component at the interface \cite{TornbergEngquist2006};
here the width is a property of the medium, smaller than the grid, and
the aim is the scheme's full order through it. We found no prior instance
of stencils on an unchanged equispaced or scattered node set whose basis
is continued through a sub-grid smooth edge by ordinary differential
equations, nor of the observation that the construction reduces to the
jump stencils as $\delta \to 0$; the search is logged in the repository's
\texttt{LITERATURE.md}. We ran the smoothing and averaging treatments on
both schemes, as our implementation of each on one problem at one
contrast (Sections~\ref{sec:results1d} and~\ref{sec:results2d}): none
reaches the seeds' order, the best of them is second order at a jump in
one dimension, and the seeds lie $3$ to $730$ times below the best
treatment there and $3$ to $6$ times below it on the scattered nodes. We
claim nothing beyond that measurement.

\subsection{One dimension}
\label{sec:setting1d}
The one-dimensional problem is the two-way wave equation in first-order
form on the periodic interval $[-1, 1)$, with $u$ the particle velocity
and $f$ the stress,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sys1d}
  \rho\, u_t = f_x, \qquad f_t = K\, u_x, \qquad K = \rho c^2,
\end{equation}
where $c(x) > 0$ is the wave speed and $\rho(x) > 0$ the density. Eliminating one
field gives a second-order operator for each,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Lops}
  u_{tt} = \Lu u, \quad \Lu = \frac{1}{\rho} \px K \px; \qquad
  f_{tt} = \Lf f, \quad \Lf = K \px \frac{1}{\rho} \px .
\end{equation}
The medium is a background material $(c, \rho) = (1, 1)$ with one layer
$[x_1, x_2) = [0, \tfrac12)$ of $(c, \rho) = (2, 1)$, so the contrast in
$K$ is $4$ and the impedance $\rho c$ doubles. With edge width
$\delta > 0$ the two edges of the layer are $\tanh$ transitions,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:profile1d}
  \begin{gathered}
  s(x) = \frac12 \sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} \Bigl[
    \tanh\frac{x - x_1 + 2m}{\delta} - \tanh\frac{x - x_2 + 2m}{\delta}
  \Bigr], \\
  c = c_{\mathrm{bg}} + (c_{\mathrm{layer}} - c_{\mathrm{bg}})\, s,
  \qquad \rho \text{ likewise},
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
summed over the periodic images so that the profile is smooth and
periodic to rounding; $\delta = 0$ is the jump. For
$\delta \ll h$ a stencil sees a jump between two of its nodes; for
$\delta \gg h$ it sees a smooth function; the case in between is the
subject of the paper.

The grid has $n$ cell-centred nodes, $x_i = -1 + (i + \tfrac12) h$ with
$h = 2 / n$, and the semi-discrete system is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:semi1d}
  \rho_i\, \dot u_i = (D_f f)_i, \qquad \dot f_i = K_i\, (D_u u)_i,
\end{equation}
with $\rho_i = \rho(x_i)$, $K_i = K(x_i)$ and $D_u$, $D_f$ two
differentiation matrices with the sparsity of the centred five-point
stencil. In the \emph{naive} scheme both are the standard fourth-order
matrix, Fornberg's weights on the monomials \cite{Fornberg1988}: the
coefficients are sampled at the nodes and nothing else is done about the
edge. In the \emph{seed} scheme the rows whose stencils see a varying
material are rebuilt as in Section~\ref{sec:seeds1d}, separately for
$u$ and for $f$ because the two fields carry different continuity
conditions; every other row is the standard one. Time stepping is the
classical fourth-order Runge--Kutta method (RK4) with
$\Delta t = 0.4\, h / c_{\max}$. Errors are relative $\ell^2$ errors in
the stress $f$ at $t = 1$ against a reference solution
(Section~\ref{sec:results1d}).

\subsection{Two dimensions}
\label{sec:setting2d}
The two-dimensional problem is the isotropic elastic wave equation in
first-order form on the doubly periodic unit square, with velocities
$(u, v)$ and stresses $(\sigma_{xx}, \sigma_{xy}, \sigma_{yy})$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sys2d}
  \begin{aligned}
    \rho\, u_t &= \px \sigma_{xx} + \py \sigma_{xy}, &
    \partial_t \sigma_{xx} &= (\lambda + 2\mu)\, u_x + \lambda\, v_y, \\
    \rho\, v_t &= \px \sigma_{xy} + \py \sigma_{yy}, &
    \partial_t \sigma_{xy} &= \mu\, (u_y + v_x), \\
    & & \partial_t \sigma_{yy} &= \lambda\, u_x + (\lambda + 2\mu)\, v_y,
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with Lam\'e parameters $\lambda, \mu > 0$ and density $\rho > 0$, P- and
S-wave speeds $c_p = \sqrt{(\lambda + 2\mu) / \rho}$ and
$c_s = \sqrt{\mu / \rho}$, and $K = \lambda + 2\mu$ in the role of
$\rho c^2$. (The implementation and the dissertation name the stresses
$f, g, h$; we write $\sigma$ so that $h$ can stay the node spacing.)
The medium is a background $(\lambda, \mu, \rho) = (1, 1, 1)$ with a band
$(4, 4, 2)$ between the two interfaces $y = y_0 + A \sin 2\pi x$,
$y_0 = \tfrac14$ and $\tfrac12$, so $c_p$ rises from $\sqrt 3$ to
$\sqrt 6$ and $c_s$ from $1$ to $\sqrt 2$ across each interface. The
flat case is $A = 0$ and the curved case $A = 0.02$, the two geometries
of the dissertation's elastic tests \cite{Martin2016}. With edge width
$\delta > 0$ each interface becomes a $\tanh$ transition in the signed
normal distance to it, positive above the curve: with $d_1$ and $d_2$ the
distances to the lower and the upper interface of one periodic image of
the band, the image whose centre line is nearest, the band's weight is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:profile2d}
  \begin{gathered}
  s = \tfrac12 \sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} \Bigl[
    \tanh\frac{d_1 + m}{\delta} - \tanh\frac{d_2 + m}{\delta}
  \Bigr], \\
  (\lambda, \mu, \rho) = (\lambda, \mu, \rho)_{\mathrm{bg}}
  + \bigl[ (\lambda, \mu, \rho)_{\mathrm{band}}
  - (\lambda, \mu, \rho)_{\mathrm{bg}} \bigr]\, s,
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
the two-dimensional form of \eqref{eq:profile1d}, so that $\lambda$,
$\mu$ and $\rho$, and hence $K$, are linear in the weight while $c_p$
and $c_s$ are not. For a flat interface $d_1$ and $d_2$ are the vertical
offsets $y - y_0$; for a curved one they are the true signed distances,
through the foot points on the curves (Section~\ref{sec:curved2d}). Two edges a gap $g$ apart reach
only $\tanh(g / 2\delta)$ of the contrast between them, so the band's
width of $\tfrac14$ bounds the edge widths we can pose; nothing here
approaches that bound.

\para{Node sets}
A node set of $n$ nodes is built as in \cite{MartinFornberg2017}: six
fixed rows of nodes hug each interface, three on each side at normal
distances $(\tfrac12 + j \tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}) h$, $j = 0, 1, 2$, with
alternate rows shifted by half a spacing so that the rows straddle the
interface in a hexagonal pattern, and every other node is placed by a
simulated electrostatic repulsion from a random start. The straddling
rows were found empirically to be what keeps the interface-aware
stencils stable, and we keep them for the seed stencils. Throughout,
$h = n^{-1/2}$ denotes the mean spacing, and convergence rates are
measured in it; the node counts are $n = 2500$, $4900$, $10{,}000$ and
$19{,}600$, so $h$ runs from $0.02$ to $0.0071$.

\para{RBF-FD}
RBF-FD \cite{FornbergFlyer2015primer,FornbergFlyer2015acta} builds each
row of a differentiation matrix from a local stencil. For a stencil of $s$
nodes $x_1, \ldots, x_s$ about an evaluation node $x_c$, the weights $w$
of a linear operator $\mathcal{L}$ solve the saddle-point system
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rbffd}
  \begin{bmatrix} A & P \\ P^{\mathsf T} & 0 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} w \\ w_* \end{bmatrix}
  =
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathcal{L}\, \varphi(|x - x_i|) \big|_{x_c} \\
    \mathcal{L}\, p_m(x) \big|_{x_c}
  \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $A_{ij} = \varphi(|x_i - x_j|)$ for the Gaussian
$\varphi(r) = e^{-(\varepsilon r)^2}$, $P_{im} = p_m(x_i)$ over the
bivariate monomials $p_m$ up to a chosen degree, evaluated in
coordinates scaled by the stencil radius, and $w_*$ discarded. The constraint rows make the weights
exact on the polynomials, which take over from the Gaussians under
refinement \cite{FlyerFornbergBayonaBarnett2016,BayonaFlyerFornbergBarnett2017},
and the shape parameter is scaled to the local spacing,
$\varepsilon = 0.4 / d$ with $d$ the distance from $x_c$ to its
third-nearest neighbour.\footnote{The published description
\cite{MartinFornberg2017} says the nearest neighbour; the original
\textsc{Matlab} and the present implementation use the third-nearest.
The choice fixes a constant in $\varepsilon h$ and nothing else.} Away
from the interfaces every stencil has $30$ nodes and is exact on the
polynomials of degree $\le 4$; stencils across a jump have $19$ nodes
and degree $\le 3$, with the piecewise basis of
Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils}. Two matrices $D_x$ and $D_y$ result, and
the $5n \times 5n$ block operator of \eqref{eq:sys2d} is assembled from
them with the material coefficients evaluated at each row's node. A
small hyperviscosity $\gamma \Delta^3$ is added to every field to move
the spurious eigenvalues of the RBF-FD operator into the left half-plane
\cite{FornbergLehto2011}: the $\Delta^3$ stencils are built on the same
nodes and the same basis as the derivative stencils, and
$\gamma = \gamma_0\, (h / h_0)^5$ with $\gamma_0 = 2.4 \times 10^{-11}$
at $h_0 = 0.02$, which keeps $\gamma \Delta^3$ proportional to $1 / h$
like the wave operator so that one CFL number serves every $n$. Time
stepping is RK4 with $\Delta t = 0.5\, h / c_{p,\max}$, capped where
necessary by the hyperviscosity spectrum.

\para{Pulses and errors}
The normal-incidence runs send a plane P-wave pulse, Gaussian in $y$
with sharpness $15$, from $y = 0.875$ in the background towards $-y$
through both interfaces; the true solution then has $u \equiv 0$, and
the largest $|u|$ a scheme produces is a clean measure of what its
stencils do at the edges. The oblique runs send a plane-wave train at
$26.6^\circ$ to the normal (direction $(1, 2)$), which mode-converts at
each interface. Errors are relative $\ell^2$ errors in $v$ at $t = 1$
against the references described with each experiment in
Section~\ref{sec:results2d}, together with the spurious $u$ at normal
incidence and the relative error in $u$ otherwise.

\subsection{Interface-aware stencils for a jump}
\label{sec:jumpstencils}
The seeds extend the interface-aware stencils of
\cite{Martin2016,MartinFornberg2017}, which we summarise in the form the
later sections use; the derivations are in those references, and the
same stencils serve parabolic interface problems in
\cite{MartinFornberg2017heat}. RBF-FD has since been applied to waves in
heterogeneous media \cite{BerljavacEtAl2021} and, at high order, to
elliptic interface problems \cite{ChengJuZhang2026}.

\para{One dimension}
Write \eqref{eq:sys1d} as $\pt (u, f)^{\mathsf T} = \mathcal{D}\, (u,
f)^{\mathsf T}$ with the block operator
\[
  \mathcal{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \rho^{-1} \px \\ K \px & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Across a material jump, $u$ and $f$ are continuous for all time,
hence so are all their time derivatives, and $\pt^k (u, f)^{\mathsf T} =
\mathcal{D}^k (u, f)^{\mathsf T}$ converts each of those conditions into
a condition on spatial derivatives, with the material of the side it is
evaluated on: $\mathcal{D}_L^k (u_L, f_L)^{\mathsf T} = \mathcal{D}_R^k
(u_R, f_R)^{\mathsf T}$ at the jump for $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ Expanding
$u$ and $f$ on each side as polynomials of degree four about the jump
and imposing these conditions for $k \le 4$ gives a square
\emph{continuity matrix} per side, $C_L a_L = C_R a_R$ on the
coefficient vectors. Keeping the monomials on one side and translating
them across with $C_R^{-1} C_L$ yields the \emph{translated basis}:
piecewise polynomials, one per monomial, that satisfy every continuity
condition. A stencil whose nodes straddle both edges of a layer thinner
than the stencil chains two translations. The stencil weights are then
the ones that differentiate every basis function exactly at the
evaluation point, a small dense solve per stencil, and the $f$ basis is
built by the same construction rather than from the $u$ basis through
the operator, which would lose an order. This is the scheme that
Section~\ref{sec:results1d} calls the jump case; the even continuity
conditions relate a field to itself and the odd ones route through the
other field, a pattern the seeds inherit.

\para{Two dimensions}
A stencil near an interface works in a local frame: origin at the
nearest interface point, $x'$ along the tangent and $y'$ along the
normal. The isotropic system \eqref{eq:sys2d} keeps its form under
rotation, so in that frame the rotated fields obey the same equations
and the interface is, locally, $y' = 0$. The velocities $(u', v')$ and
the tractions $(\sigma'_{x'y'}, \sigma'_{y'y'})$ are continuous across
it for all time; the remaining stress $\sigma'_{x'x'}$ may jump. Expand
$(u', v')$ on each side in the monomials of $(x', y')$ up to degree
four, convert the time derivatives of the continuous quantities through
powers of the block operator (even powers relate velocities to
velocities, odd powers route through the stresses), and match the
coefficients of the powers of $x'$ at each order: a square continuity
matrix per side, a translated velocity basis, truncated to degree three.
The stress basis is generated from it by applying the stress rows of the
operator on each side and dropping the three columns that come from
rigid motions, $27$ functions in all. The RBF-FD solve
\eqref{eq:rbffd} then couples the fields: the stress rates need weights
on $u'$ and $v'$ data jointly, the velocity rates weights on the three
stresses jointly, with the Gaussian block repeated on the diagonal and
the piecewise basis in place of $P$. The weights are rotated back to the
laboratory frame, and the hyperviscosity stencils across the interface
use the same piecewise basis, which the original work found necessary
for accuracy. The interface is treated as locally flat in the frame;
carrying curvature into the continuity conditions by expanding the
interface in $x'$ is possible \cite{MartinFornberg2017} but was not used
by the original code and is not used here. This is the approximation we
call zeroth order in the curvature, and the curved seeds of
Section~\ref{sec:seeds2d} make the same one.

\subsection{Scope of the implementation}
\label{sec:scope}
The experiments run on a Python reimplementation of the 2016--2017
methods (NumPy and SciPy, with a test for every numerical routine) that
reproduces the constructions above and restructures the code. Of the
original two-dimensional code it leaves out three things: the
\emph{triple stencils} that see both interfaces of a band thinner than
one stencil (the band here is $\tfrac14$ wide and none is needed), the
variable Lam\'e parameters inside the band of the published test case
(the band here is a constant material), and the finite-difference far
field of the FD/RBF-FD hybrid (every stencil here is RBF-FD). Point
sources and the Marmousi model are out of scope. In one dimension the
implementation takes piecewise-constant materials, with the smooth edges
of \eqref{eq:profile1d}, and not the dissertation's smoothly varying
background, because the exact reference of the jump case, a ray sum,
needs constant pieces; the published treatment of a wave speed that
varies smoothly up to an interface, a degree-four Taylor expansion of
$c$ about the interface point \cite{Martin2016,MartinFornberg2017}, is
the Taylor route of Section~\ref{sec:taylor} and is not ported. The bulk scheme is fourth order (FD4 in one dimension, degree four in
two); the rows across an edge are degree three in two dimensions, third
order in the first derivatives, in a band about $4h$ wide for a jump and
about $19\delta$ wide for a smooth edge (Section~\ref{sec:curvedresults}
measures what the band's width costs); everything is at one material
contrast. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to both restrictions.

\subsection{Seeds as the profiles of solutions polynomial in time}
\label{sec:chain}
Consider the velocity field with its operator $\Lu$ of \eqref{eq:Lops}
and look for solutions of $u_{tt} = \Lu u$ that are polynomial in time,
$u(x, t) = \sum_{j = 0}^{J} t^j g_j(x)$. Matching powers of $t$ gives
$(j + 2)(j + 1)\, g_{j+2} = \Lu g_j$, so the two top coefficients lie in
$\ker \Lu$ and the $t = 0$ profile $g_0$ satisfies $\Lu^m g_0 = 0$ for
some $m$. With constant coefficients $\Lu = c^2 \px^2$ and
$\ker \px^{2m}$ is exactly the polynomials of degree $< 2m$: the
profiles of time-polynomial solutions are what plays the role of
polynomials for the operator. We fix a basis of them by anchoring at the
evaluation point.

\begin{definition}[Seeds]
\label{def:seeds}
Let $x_e$ be the evaluation point, $c_e = c(x_e)$ and $K_e = K(x_e)$.
The \emph{seeds} of the $u$ field at $x_e$ are the functions
$\phi_0, \phi_1, \phi_2, \ldots$ with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:chain}
  \begin{gathered}
  \phi_0 = 1, \qquad K \phi_1' = K_e, \quad \phi_1(x_e) = 0, \\
  \Lu \phi_k = k (k - 1)\, c_e^2\, \phi_{k-2}, \quad
  \phi_k(x_e) = \phi_k'(x_e) = 0 \quad (k \ge 2).
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The seeds of the $f$ field are defined by the same chain with $\Lf$ in
place of $\Lu$ and $\phi_1' / \rho = 1 / \rho_e$ in place of the flux
condition.
\end{definition}

Each seed is the profile of one time-polynomial solution, and the
normalisation is chosen so that $\phi_k = (x - x_e)^k$ when the
coefficients are constant: $\Lu (x - x_e)^k = c^2 k (k - 1) (x - x_e)^{k-2}$,
and the anchor conditions fix the two integration constants. Read one at
a time, the chain says what each monomial becomes through the edge.
The constant solution $u \equiv C$ gives $\phi_0$. A solution linear in
time, $u = g_0 + t g_1$ with $u_t = g_1 = C$, forces $\Lu g_0 = 0$, that
is $K g_0' = \mathrm{const}$: the $x$-like seed $\phi_1$ is the profile
of \emph{constant flux}, $\phi_1(x) = K_e \int_{x_e}^x \mathrm{d}\xi /
K(\xi)$, and through the other field this says that $f_t = K u_x$ is
constant in $x$. It is the flux that stays smooth through the edge, not
the slope. A solution with $u_{tt} = C$ forces $\Lu g_0 = C$, and
$C = 2 c_e^2$ with the anchor conditions gives the $x^2$-like seed; here
$C \ne 0$ matters, since $C = 0$ would only return $\ker \Lu$. The cubic
condition $u_{ttt} = C$ fixes the profile of $u_t$, not of $u$, so
$\phi_3$ is the profile whose second time derivative is $x$-like,
$\Lu \phi_3 = 6 c_e^2 \phi_1$; through the other field,
$f_{ttt} = K (u_{tt})_x = 6 c_e^2 K_e$ is constant. The even seeds are
thus the conditions $\pt^k u = \mathrm{const}$ and the odd seeds the
conditions $\pt^k f = \mathrm{const}$, the same alternation between the
fields as in the continuity conditions of the jump case, where the even
conditions relate a field to itself and the odd ones route through the
other field.

\para{First-order form}
The chain is integrated as a first-order system that never
differentiates the coefficients. For the $u$ seeds the state is
$(\phi_k, \psi_k)$ with $\psi_k = K \phi_k'$ the flux,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:firstorder}
  \phi_k' = \psi_k / K, \qquad
  \psi_k' = \rho\, k (k - 1)\, c_e^2\, \phi_{k-2},
\end{equation}
with initial data $\phi_0(x_e) = 1$, $\psi_1(x_e) = K_e$ and zero
otherwise; for the $f$ seeds the state is $(\phi_k, \psi_k)$ with
$\psi_k = \phi_k' / \rho$, $\phi_k' = \rho \psi_k$,
$\psi_k' = k (k - 1)\, c_e^2\, \phi_{k-2} / K$, and $\psi_1(x_e) = 1 /
\rho_e$. All $p$ seeds of a field march together, from $x_e$ outward to
each side of the stencil, since the right-hand side of $\phi_k$ only
involves $\phi_{k-2}$. In the stencil coordinate $\xi = (x - x_e) / h_s$,
with $h_s$ the stencil half-width, the chain reads the same with $\px$
replaced by $\partial_\xi$, and then $\phi_k \approx \xi^k$: the
interpolation matrix $A_{kj} = \phi_k(\xi_j)$ is a small perturbed
Vandermonde matrix, and its condition number stays between $20$ and
$60$ for $\delta$ from $10^{-5}$ to $1$ in the setting of
Section~\ref{sec:setting1d}. Only $\phi_1$ has a non-zero derivative at
the anchor, so the weights solve $A \tilde w = e_1$ and $w = \tilde w /
h_s$. Figure~\ref{fig:seeds1d} shows the $u$ seeds of one stencil for
three edge widths.

\subsection{Nested kernels and the jump as the limit}
\label{sec:jumplimit}
Only the span of the seeds enters the weights, and the spans are
canonical. Since $\Lu$ sends each seed two steps down the chain,
$\operatorname{span}\{\phi_0, \ldots, \phi_k\}$ is the $(k + 1)$-th
space of
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:nested}
  \{1\} \subset \ker \Lu \subset \{ \Lu g = \mathrm{const} \}
  \subset \ker \Lu^2 \subset \{ \Lu^2 g = \mathrm{const} \}
  \subset \cdots,
\end{equation}
where $\{ \Lu g = \mathrm{const} \}$ is short for the space of $g$ with
$\Lu g$ constant, so that a different anchor or normalisation changes
the basis and not the space, and the weights not at all.

For a jump, read $\Lu$ in the weak sense: $\phi$ and the flux $K \phi'$
continuous across it. Then $\ker \Lu$ is spanned by $1$ and a
piecewise-linear function whose slopes are in the ratio $K_L / K_R$,
which is the translated $x$ of Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils}, the
function that keeps $\rho c^2 u_x$ continuous; $\Lu \phi_2 = 2 c_e^2$
gives $\phi_2'' = 2 c_e^2 / c^2$ on each side, which is the continuity
of $u_{tt}$; and so on up the chain, each condition $\pt^k u =
\mathrm{const}$ or $\pt^k f = \mathrm{const}$ being one of the
continuity conditions the translated basis was built from. The chain
for a jump is therefore the translated Taylor basis, and the
interface-aware stencils are the seed stencils at $\delta = 0$: one
construction with two implementations, algebra for a jump and an ODE
march for a smooth edge. The limit is approached at first order in
$\delta$. For a stencil of half-width $0.02$ straddling an edge of the
default contrast, the seed weights differ from the interface-aware
weights by $22\%$, $10\%$, $0.98\%$ and $0.098\%$ of the largest weight
at $\delta = 10^{-2}$, $10^{-3}$, $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-5}$, for both
fields, and equally for a stencil that straddles both edges of a layer
thinner than itself, the double-cross of the jump case, which the march
handles by passing through both edges.

\subsection{Well-posedness}
\label{sec:ect}
The per-stencil solve $A \tilde w = e_1$ is nonsingular for every edge
width, including the jump, and not only for the node sets we happened to
use.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:ect}
Let $K$ and $\rho$ be positive and continuous on an interval $I$
containing $x_e$ (or positive and piecewise continuous with finitely
many jumps, none at $x_e$, with $\Lu$ read in the weak sense). Then for every $k$ the seeds $\phi_0, \ldots,
\phi_k$ of Definition~\ref{def:seeds} form an extended complete
Chebyshev system on $I$: interpolation in their span at any $k + 1$
distinct points of $I$ is uniquely solvable. The same holds for the $f$
seeds.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each space in \eqref{eq:nested} is the kernel of an operator in P\'olya
form, a product of $\px$ and multiplications by positive functions:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:polya}
  \begin{aligned}
  \rho\, \Lu^m &= \px K \px\, \tfrac{1}{\rho}\, \px K \px \cdots \px K \px
  &&(2m \text{ derivatives}), \\
  \px \Lu^m &= \px\, \tfrac{1}{\rho}\, \px K \px \cdots \px K \px
  &&(2m + 1 \text{ derivatives}),
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\ker \Lu^m = \ker (\rho \Lu^m)$ the even spaces and $\{ g :
\Lu^m g = \mathrm{const} \} = \ker (\px \Lu^m)$ the odd ones; for $\Lf$
the roles of $K$ and $1 / \rho$ are swapped. The kernel of such an
operator with positive weights $w_0, w_1, \ldots$ has the nested basis
$w_0$, $w_0 \int w_1$, $w_0 \int w_1 \int w_2$, \ldots, and is an
extended complete Chebyshev system on any interval where the weights are
positive \cite{Polya1922,KarlinStudden1966,Coppel1971}; in particular it
has the Haar property, so interpolation at distinct points in it is
uniquely solvable. It remains to see that the seeds span these kernels.
Their number matches the order of the operator, and they are
independent because their leading behaviour at $x_e$ is triangular: by
induction on the chain, $\phi_{k-2} = c_{k-2} (x - x_e)^{k-2} (1 + o(1))$
with $c_{k-2} \ne 0$, and integrating $(K \phi_k')' = \rho\, k (k - 1)
c_e^2 \phi_{k-2}$ twice from $x_e$ with the anchor conditions, $K$ and
$\rho$ positive and continuous there, gives $\phi_k = c_k (x - x_e)^k
(1 + o(1))$ with $c_k \ne 0$; no derivative beyond those of the
first-order system \eqref{eq:firstorder} is used. For the weak reading,
the integrals in the nested basis are unaffected by finitely many jumps
of the weights away from $x_e$, and the argument is the same.
\end{proof}

Interpolation in an extended complete Chebyshev system also has a Newton
form with a remainder in which the operator that annihilates the space
takes the place of the $(k + 1)$-th derivative (the generalised divided
differences of \cite{Muhlbach1973}). For the five-point stencil the
annihilator of $\operatorname{span}\{\phi_0, \ldots, \phi_4\}$ is
$\px \Lu^2$, so the truncation error of the seed stencil on a function
$g$ is $O(h^4\, \px \Lu^2 g)$, with a constant the remainder formula
expresses through the weights $K$ and $1 / \rho$ and does not bound
uniformly in $\delta / h$. On a \emph{solution} of the wave equation,
$\Lu u = u_{tt}$ and hence $\px \Lu^2 u = \px \pt^4 u$, the
$x$-derivative of another solution, in place of FD4's $u^{(5)}$, which is
$\sim \delta^{-4}$ inside the edge: the seed stencil's error is driven by
a quantity of the solution's own size, where $u_x$ changes by $O(1)$
across the edge whatever its width and it is the flux $K u_x$ that is
smooth. Whether the constant is in fact independent of $\delta$ is the
prediction that Section~\ref{sec:results1d} tests, not a consequence of
Proposition~\ref{prop:ect}: standard FD4 converges at fourth order only
once $h \lesssim \delta$ and looks low-order above that, with a knee at
$h \approx \delta$; the seed stencils converge at fourth order with a
constant that does not depend on $\delta$.

\begin{remark}
The seeds are close relatives of two classical objects. Splines whose
pieces lie in $\ker L$ for a differential operator $L$ are the
L-splines of \cite{SchultzVarga1967}, and the harmonic-mean coefficient
$h / \int \mathrm{d}x / K$ of the conservative difference schemes of
\cite{TikhonovSamarskii1962} is the two-point version of $\phi_1$.
Section~\ref{sec:priorwork} places the construction among them.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Why the Taylor route does not reach a sub-grid edge}
\label{sec:taylor}
The interface-aware construction of Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils} can
take smoothly varying coefficients: the continuity conditions are built
from multiplication matrices formed from the Taylor coefficients of
$1 / \rho$ and $K$ about the interface \cite{Martin2016,MartinFornberg2017}, and polynomial
quasi-Trefftz spaces \cite{ImbertGerardMoiolaStocker2023} do the same in
general, with basis functions that satisfy the variable-coefficient
equation to Taylor order at a point. For a sub-grid edge this route
cannot work, and the reason is not the algebra. The profile
$\tanh(x / \delta)$ has poles at $\pm i \pi \delta / 2$, so its Taylor
series about the edge converges only within a distance $\pi \delta / 2$
of it, while the stencil reaches $2h \gg \delta$; no polynomial degree
that the stencil could carry represents the coefficients across it. The
ODE march is what a sub-grid edge needs. For a resolved edge,
$\delta \gg h$, the seed weights still differ from Fornberg's at first
order in $h / \delta$, by $17\%$ of the largest weight at $\delta = 8h$
for the default contrast, because the seeds are exact for a different
five-dimensional space; both stencils are fourth order on solutions
there, and Section~\ref{sec:results1d} finds them indistinguishable end
to end.

\subsection{Stencil assembly and cost}
\label{sec:assembly1d}
The seed scheme rebuilds every row of $D_u$ and $D_f$ whose stencil
nodes see different material values, and leaves every other row the
standard one. For a $\tanh$ edge that region reaches about $19\delta$
from each edge centre, where the tails round to the far-field value in
double precision, plus the stencil's reach. In each rebuilt row the
$2p$ states of \eqref{eq:firstorder} for each field are integrated from $\xi = 0$
outward to each side with an explicit adaptive Runge--Kutta method of
order eight, at relative tolerance $10^{-13}$ and absolute tolerance
$10^{-15}$, with segment boundaries at the edge centres and at
$\pm 10\delta$ from them, so that the adaptive step never has to
discover the edge in the middle of a long step across flat material.
One march serves both fields and all $p$ seeds, and a stencil that
straddles both edges of a thin layer needs nothing further. The two
$p \times p$ solves follow. In the reference implementation a march
costs $5$ to $15$\,ms, and the $n = 1600$ operator at $\delta = 0.01$
takes $2.3$\,s to build: seconds rather than milliseconds, fine for a
study and not tuned. Two remarks on the range of $\delta$. Since the
seeds converge to the translated basis at first order in $\delta$, an
edge thinner than about $10^{-5} h$ is better served by the
interface-aware weights directly, the two sets of weights then differing
by less than any discretisation error of interest while the march would
be resolving a profile $10^5$ times thinner than the stencil; and for $\delta$ of the order of the stencil or wider
every row is rebuilt and the standard weights would have done as well
(Section~\ref{sec:taylor}), so the rule of when to seed, settled in two
dimensions in Section~\ref{sec:results2d}, is safe here too, and
conservative: in one dimension seeding a resolved edge costs nothing
(Section~\ref{sec:results1d}).

The experiment of this section is the one-dimensional test of the
prediction that closes Section~\ref{sec:ect}: standard FD4 loses its order
through an edge it does not resolve and recovers it at $h \approx
\delta$, while the seed stencils on the same grid are fourth order at
every resolution with a constant that does not depend on $\delta$.

\para{Test problem}
The medium is that of Section~\ref{sec:setting1d}, the layer $[0,
\tfrac12)$ with $c$ from $1$ to $2$ at $\rho = 1$, so that $K$ quadruples
and the impedance doubles across each edge, with edges of width $\delta
\in \{0, 0.0025, 0.01, 0.04\}$. The initial condition is a right-going
Gaussian stress pulse, $f = \exp(-60\, d(x)^2)$ with $d(x)$ the periodic
distance from $x$ to $-0.6$ (so $f$ is periodic and smooth to rounding)
and $u = -f / \rho c$, started in the background far enough from the
layer that the tails of the reference below are clean. Its $e^{-1}$ width is $2 / \sqrt{60}
\approx 0.26$, thirteen spacings on the coarsest grid, so the pulse is
resolved on every grid and the edges are the only thing under test. The
grids have $n = 100$, $200$, $400$, $800$ and $1600$ nodes, $h = 0.02$
down to $0.00125$; for $\delta = 0.0025$ the ratio $h / \delta$ runs from
$8$ to $\tfrac12$, for $\delta = 0.01$ from $2$ to $\tfrac18$, and for
$\delta = 0.04$ every grid resolves the edge, $h / \delta \le \tfrac12$.
Both schemes step with RK4 at $\Delta t = 0.4\, h / c_{\max}$ to $t = 1$,
by which time the pulse has crossed the layer: the transmitted pulse is
in the background beyond the layer, the reflection from the lower edge
is on its way back, and the reflection from the upper edge is still
inside the layer (Figure~\ref{fig:snapshot1d}). The error is the relative
$\ell^2$ error in $f$ over the whole grid at $t = 1$.

\para{References}
For the jump the reference is the exact solution, a ray sum over the
reflections and transmissions at the two interfaces.
For $\delta > 0$ the coefficients are smooth and periodic and the
reference is a Fourier pseudo-spectral discretisation of
\eqref{eq:sys1d}: derivatives in Fourier space, the coefficients applied
pointwise, the same RK4 loop. It shares nothing with the finite-difference
stencils under test, and the semi-discretisation conserves the energy
$\int (f^2 / K + \rho u^2)\, \mathrm{d}x$ exactly, the Fourier derivative
being skew-symmetric. Its resolution is set by the edge: on the interval
of period $2$, $\tanh(x / \delta)$ has poles at $\pm i \pi \delta / 2$,
so its Fourier coefficients decay like $e^{-\pi^2 \delta m / 2}$ in the
mode number $m$, and the grid is the power of two that carries every mode
down to $10^{-14}$: $8192$ nodes for $\delta = 0.0025$, $2048$ for
$0.01$, and the floor of $1024$ for $0.04$. With $\Delta t = 5 \times
10^{-5}$ the reference is accurate to about $10^{-10}$, below every error
in Table~\ref{tab:knee1d} by more than two orders of magnitude, and is
interpolated to the test grids trigonometrically.

\para{The knee}
Table~\ref{tab:knee1d} and Figure~\ref{fig:knee1d} give the errors and
the rates between successive grids. For the jump the picture is the known
one \cite{Martin2016,MartinFornberg2017}: standard FD4 is first order and
the interface-aware stencils are fourth order, $3.9 \times 10^{-3}$ down
to $6.2 \times 10^{-8}$. Through the edge of width $\delta = 0.0025$
standard FD4 converges at rates $1.3$ and $2.2$ while $h > \delta$, then
drops by a factor $200$ between $n = 400$ and $n = 800$, from $h =
2\delta$ to $h = \delta$: the knee. Over the two doublings past $h =
2\delta$ its error falls by a factor $850$, an average rate of $4.9$,
unevenly ($7.7$, then $2.0$), because the nodes sample the $\tanh$ at
positions that move with $n$ and so does the jump the stencil
effectively sees; this is also why the rates before the knee are not the
clean $1.0$ of the jump. For $\delta = 0.01$ the knee sits between
$n = 100$ and $200$, again at $h \approx \delta$, and the rates after it
are $4.0$, $5.0$ and $4.0$. For $\delta = 0.04$, which every grid
resolves, there is no knee and standard FD4 is fourth order throughout.

\para{Conditioning and stability}
The $5 \times 5$ interpolation matrices of the rebuilt rows are the
perturbed Vandermonde matrices of Section~\ref{sec:chain}, with condition
numbers between $20$ and $60$ for $\delta$ from $10^{-5}$ to $1$; nothing
in the sweep approaches the range of $\delta$ where the interface-aware
weights should be used directly. The semi-discrete operator of the seed scheme has no growing
modes to speak of: the largest real part of its spectrum is
$8 \times 10^{-8}$ at $\delta = 0.005$ on $400$ nodes and about
$10^{-13}$ in the other cases examined, and RK4 at $\Delta t = 0.4\, h /
c_{\max}$ was stable in every run of the sweep. The runs cost seconds
(Section~\ref{sec:assembly1d}). Nothing was tried at sharper contrasts,
for layers thinner than the $19\delta$ tails of an edge, or at orders
above four; the construction does not change for any of them, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists them among the limitations.

\para{The standing alternative: the standard scheme on a changed medium}
% TRACE: notes §2.1 (#69). Tornberg–Engquist 2006 §4.1, eq. 18–22 and §5,
% eq. 37–39 (papers/ index); Koene et al. 2022 §3.2–3.3. Numbers from
% paper/data/wave1d_stiff.json (modes widen1, widen2, cell, cell2,
% bandlimit); the ratios quoted are computed from the cache.
In seismic and electromagnetic finite differences an interface is
routinely handled by changing the medium rather than the stencil
(Section~\ref{sec:priorwork}). Tornberg and Engquist
\cite{TornbergEngquist2006} derive the change for the system
$p_t = a u_x$, $u_t = b p_x$ with $a = -\rho c^2$ and $b = -1/\rho$:
$1/a$ and $1/b$ linear across the one cell centred on the jump, that is,
the compliance $1/K$ and the density replaced by their cell means, the
harmonic and arithmetic means of the Moczo line \cite{MoczoEtAl2002};
their Theorem~1 gives second order for the Yee scheme, regularising $a$
itself instead of $1/a$ stays first order with a smaller constant, and
their fourth-order staggered scheme reaches second order only with its
temporal correction terms masked near the jump, terms the collocated
scheme here does not have. Koene, Wittsten and Robertsson
\cite{KoeneWittstenRobertsson2022} instead band-limit the density and
the compliance to the grid Nyquist with a windowed low-pass filter. We
ran both on the scheme of this section: the same FD4 and RK4, grids,
pulse and true-$\delta$ reference as Table~\ref{tab:knee1d}, with the
coefficients the standard scheme samples replaced by their cell means
over one cell and over two (the layer's edges sit on cell boundaries of
every grid here, so the one-cell ramp of a jump ends exactly at the
nearest nodes and changes nothing at $\delta = 0$; a staggered grid
always has a coefficient position within $h/4$ of a jump, a collocated
one does not), by the band-limited coefficients (cutoff $1.1\pi/h$,
Hanning window of half-width $2.5h$, their filter applied to the true
profile), and, as the coarse bound, by the true profile with its edge
widened to $\max(\delta, h)$ and $\max(\delta, 2h)$, which blends $c$
and $\rho$ and is the case they warn about. Each treatment is applied at
its prescribed width at every $n$: the sources have no $\delta$ and state
no switch. Table~\ref{tab:comparators1d} and
Figure~\ref{fig:comparators1d} give the result.

At the jump no coefficient treatment lifts the collocated scheme's first
order except the two-cell mean, which is second order at every $n$,
$5.5\times$ below the sampled medium at $n = 100$ and $87\times$ at
$n = 1600$; the interface-aware stencils are $3.3\times$ below it on the
coarsest grid and $730\times$ on the finest. The band-limited medium
keeps first order at $0.8\times$ the constant, the widened edges at
$1.4\times$ and $2.7\times$. Through the unresolved edge
$\delta = 0.0025$ the one-cell mean and the band-limited medium cut the
sampled error by $1.25$ to $1.6\times$ while $h > \delta$ and follow it
through the knee; the two-cell mean stays second order, $5.7$ to
$11\times$ below the sampled medium while $h \ge 2\delta$ and $4.7$ to
$4.9\times$ above it from $h = \delta$ on, where the sampled scheme is
fourth order and the averaged medium is not the true one. The seeds are
$3.2\times$ below the two-cell mean at $h = 8\delta$, $37\times$ at
$h = 2\delta$, $146\times$ at $h = \delta$ and $580\times$ at
$h = \delta / 2$: the gap widens toward the knee. Widening the edge is
worse than sampling it wherever it acts ($1.03$ to $1.7\times$ for one
cell; $2.2$ to $5.1\times$ for two while $h \ge 2\delta$ and $350\times$
at $h = \delta$, where the two-cell edge is still twice the true one) and
equal to it once the true edge is the wider one. On a resolved edge every averaging or filtering treatment at its
prescribed width is second order and falls behind the fourth-order
sampled scheme, by $47$ to $530\times$ at $n = 1600$ through
$\delta = 0.01$ and $0.04$, while the widened edge, no longer wider than
the true one, is the sampled scheme itself: the
second-order component the literature describes, measured on this
scheme. The treatments therefore need the same switch as the seeds,
applied while $h > \delta$ and not otherwise, which their sources, written
for jumps, do not state; with it the best of them is second order through
the edge at no cost per stencil, and the seeds are fourth order at one
ODE march per rebuilt row. What this licenses is stated in
Section~\ref{sec:priorwork}: one scheme, one contrast, one pulse, our
implementation of each treatment on a collocated grid, against sources
that analyse and run them on staggered grids.

The chain of Section~\ref{sec:chain} is dimension-free: for any operator
$\mathcal{L}$ the nested spaces $\{1\} \subset \ker \mathcal{L} \subset
\{\mathcal{L} g = \mathrm{const}\} \subset \ker \mathcal{L}^2 \subset
\cdots$ are what they were. What is not free is the choice of basis. In
two dimensions $\ker \mathcal{L}$ is infinite-dimensional, and a space of
``polynomial-like functions of degree $\le q$'' that reduces to the
polynomials for constant coefficients needs a rule for which
$\mathcal{L}$-harmonic functions play the role of $1$, $x$, $y$, $xy$,
$x^2 - y^2, \ldots$: each harmonic polynomial needs a continuation through
the edge that looks like it away from the edge. For a straight edge that
rule is an ansatz, and it turns the problem back into ordinary
differential equations in the normal coordinate. This section constructs
the seeds of the elastic system for a straight edge, assembles them into
stencils, and says what a curved edge does to the construction.

\subsection{Design: a straight edge}
\label{sec:design2d}
A stencil that sees an edge works in the local frame of
Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils}: origin at the nearest point of the edge
centre, $x$ along the tangent and $y$ along the normal, the rotated
fields obeying \eqref{eq:sys2d} unchanged. In this section $(x, y)$ are
those local coordinates and $(u, v)$ and $\sigma$ the rotated fields, the
primes of Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils} dropped. The material depends on
$y$ alone: exactly, for a flat edge, and for a curved one in the
approximation of Section~\ref{sec:curved2d}. Eliminating the stresses
from \eqref{eq:sys2d}, with $K = \lambda + 2\mu$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:L2d}
  \begin{aligned}
  \rho\, u_{tt} &= \px \bigl[ K u_x + \lambda v_y \bigr]
    + \py \bigl[ \mu (u_y + v_x) \bigr], \\
  \rho\, v_{tt} &= \px \bigl[ \mu (u_y + v_x) \bigr]
    + \py \bigl[ \lambda u_x + K v_y \bigr],
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and $\mathcal{L}(u, v)$ denotes the right-hand side divided by $\rho$.
The three brackets are the stress rates of \eqref{eq:sys2d}, and the two
that carry $\py$ are the tractions on the edge, $\sigma_{xy}$ and
$\sigma_{yy}$, which are what stays continuous across a jump. That is the
2-D form of the observation that drove the 1-D construction: the flux
$K \phi'$, not the slope, is the smooth quantity through the edge.

\begin{proposition}[Reduction to the normal coordinate]
\label{prop:reduction}
Let $\lambda, \mu, \rho$ depend on $y$ only, and let $u = \sum_{j=0}^{q}
a_j(y)\, x^j$ and $v = \sum_{j=0}^{q} b_j(y)\, x^j$. Write the
$x^j$-coefficients of the three stress rates as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:stressrates}
  \begin{gathered}
  F_j = K (j + 1)\, a_{j+1} + \lambda\, b_j', \qquad
  \tau_j = \mu \bigl( a_j' + (j + 1)\, b_{j+1} \bigr), \\
  \nu_j = \lambda (j + 1)\, a_{j+1} + K\, b_j',
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
for $\sigma_{xx}$, $\sigma_{xy}$ and $\sigma_{yy}$ respectively, with
$a_j = b_j = 0$ for $j > q$. Then $\rho \mathcal{L}(u, v)$ is a
polynomial in $x$ of degree $\le q$ whose $x^j$-coefficients are
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Lcoeffs}
  \rho\, \mathcal{L}_{1, j} = (j + 1) F_{j+1} + \tau_j', \qquad
  \rho\, \mathcal{L}_{2, j} = (j + 1) \tau_{j+1} + \nu_j' .
\end{equation}
In particular the coefficients at level $j$ involve $(a, b)$ at levels
$j$, $j + 1$ and $j + 2$ only, so an equation $\mathcal{L}(u, v) = (\text{given})$
is a triangular system, from the top $x$-degree down, of two-component
second-order ODEs in $y$ for the pairs $(a_j, b_j)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Each bracket in \eqref{eq:L2d} is a polynomial in $x$ with coefficient
functions of $y$, as displayed in \eqref{eq:stressrates}; $\px$ shifts the
index and multiplies by it, $\py$ differentiates the coefficient, and
\eqref{eq:Lcoeffs} follows by reading off. Expanding $F_{j+1}$ gives
$\rho \mathcal{L}_{1,j} = (j + 1)(j + 2) K a_{j+2} + (j + 1) \lambda
b_{j+1}' + \tau_j'$, which reaches level $j + 2$; $\tau_j'$ and $\nu_j'$
involve $a_j''$, $b_j''$ and $b_{j+1}'$, $a_{j+1}'$, and nothing lower.
\end{proof}

\para{First-order form}
As in one dimension, the chain is integrated as a first-order system that
never differentiates the coefficients, and the tractions are the flux
variables. Solving \eqref{eq:stressrates} for the slopes and
\eqref{eq:Lcoeffs} for the derivatives of the tractions, the state at
level $j$ is $(a_j, b_j, \tau_j, \nu_j)$ with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:firstorder2d}
  \begin{aligned}
  a_j' &= \tau_j / \mu - (j + 1)\, b_{j+1}, \\
  b_j' &= \bigl( \nu_j - \lambda (j + 1)\, a_{j+1} \bigr) / K, \\
  \tau_j' &= \rho\, R_{1, j} - K (j + 2)(j + 1)\, a_{j+2}
    - \lambda (j + 1)\, b_{j+1}', \\
  \nu_j' &= \rho\, R_{2, j} - (j + 1)\, \tau_{j+1},
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $b_{j+1}'$ in the third line is the second line at level $j + 1$
and $(R_1, R_2)$ is the right-hand side the chain prescribes, next. The
coefficients $\lambda$, $\mu$, $\rho$ are only ever evaluated. The pair
$(\tau_j, \nu_j)$ is the 2-D twin of $\psi_k = K \phi_k'$ in
\eqref{eq:firstorder}.

\subsection{The elastic seeds}
\label{sec:elasticseeds}
With constant coefficients $\mathcal{L}$ maps a pair of monomials to
pairs of lower degree,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:Lmono}
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{L} (x^a y^b, 0) &= \frac{1}{\rho} \begin{pmatrix}
    K a (a - 1)\, x^{a-2} y^b + \mu\, b (b - 1)\, x^a y^{b-2} \\
    (\lambda + \mu)\, a b\, x^{a-1} y^{b-1} \end{pmatrix}, \\[4pt]
  \mathcal{L} (0, x^a y^b) &= \frac{1}{\rho} \begin{pmatrix}
    (\lambda + \mu)\, a b\, x^{a-1} y^{b-1} \\
    \mu\, a (a - 1)\, x^{a-2} y^b + K b (b - 1)\, x^a y^{b-2} \end{pmatrix},
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
that is, a matrix $C$ over the $2m$ monomial pairs $e$ ($m$ monomials to
degree $q$, the $u$-block then the $v$-block), $\mathcal{L} e =
\sum_{e'} C[e', e]\, e'$, which lowers the degree by two and is therefore
strictly triangular in degree. It is the $(u, v)$ block of the square of
the block operator of \eqref{eq:sys2d} on polynomial coefficients, the
same object the continuity matrices of Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils}
are built from.

\begin{definition}[Elastic seeds of a straight edge]
\label{def:seeds2d}
Let the evaluation node be at $(x_e, y_e)$ in the local frame and let
$C$ be the matrix \eqref{eq:Lmono} for the material at $y_e$. The
\emph{seeds} $S_e$, one per monomial pair $e$, are the solutions of
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:chain2d}
  \mathcal{L} S_e = \sum_{e'} C[e', e]\, S_{e'}
\end{equation}
of the form of Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} in $(x - x_e, y - y_e)$,
with the monomial's jet as initial data at $y = y_e$: for $e = (x^\alpha
y^\beta, 0)$, $a_\alpha(y_e) = 1$ if $\beta = 0$, $a_\alpha'(y_e) = 1$ if
$\beta = 1$, and every other $a_j$, $b_j$ and slope zero at $y_e$, with
the mirror image for $e = (0, x^\alpha y^\beta)$; the tractions at $y_e$
follow from the jet and the material there through
\eqref{eq:stressrates}.
\end{definition}

This is Definition~\ref{def:seeds} read in two dimensions: $\Lu \phi_k
= k (k - 1)\, c_e^2\, \phi_{k-2}$ is \eqref{eq:chain2d} with $C$ the
1-D chain matrix, and the anchor conditions are the jet. The seeds of
the lower monomials stand on the right, not the monomials, which is what
makes the system consistent: by the triangularity of $C$ a seed only
ever needs seeds two degrees down, and all $2m$ of them march together
as one linear system. With constant coefficients the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:chain2d} is $\mathcal{L}$ of the monomial itself, by induction
on the degree, and the linear ODE with that data has one solution, the
monomial: the seeds are the monomials, to $2.5 \times 10^{-14}$ in the
implementation's test. For the seed of $(x^\alpha y^\beta, \cdot)$ only
the levels $j \le \alpha$ are ever non-zero, since nothing above them is
driven, so a seed keeps the $x$-degree of its monomial and the medium
enters through the $y$-structure alone. Two seeds are in closed form
through any edge: $(y, 0)$ becomes $\bigl( \int_{y_e}^{y} \mu_e / \mu
\, \mathrm{d}\eta,\, 0 \bigr)$ and $(0, x)$ becomes $\bigl(
\int_{y_e}^{y} (\mu_e / \mu - 1)\, \mathrm{d}\eta,\, x \bigr)$, both with
the constant shear traction $\mu_e$ and no other stress. That is the
twin of $\phi_1 = K_e \int \mathrm{d}\xi / K$: the traction is what stays
smooth, not the slope. Figure~\ref{fig:seeds2d} shows cross-sections along the
normal of four seeds of one stencil for three edge widths, against the
monomials.

\para{The system for degree three}
The stencils across an edge are exact on velocities to degree $p = 3$,
and the stress basis is generated from velocities one degree higher, as
in the jump case, so the seeds are marched for the monomials to degree
$q = 4$: $15$ monomials per component, $30$ seeds, $x$-degrees $0$ to
$4$, and five levels of $(a_j, b_j, \tau_j, \nu_j)$ per seed, a linear
system of $600$ states. It is integrated with the same explicit
eighth-order Runge--Kutta method and tolerances as in one dimension,
from the anchor outward to each side, restarted at every node's normal
coordinate so that the node values are integrated rather than
interpolated, and at the edge centres and their $\pm 10\delta$ flanks.
The flanks matter here as they did in one dimension: a single solve
without them differs from the segmented march by $4 \times 10^{-10}$ at
$\delta = 10^{-4}$, where the adaptive step has to discover the edge.

\para{Stress seeds and rigid motions}
The stress seed of a velocity seed has the components $\sigma_{xx} =
\sum_j F_j x^j$, $\sigma_{xy} = \sum_j \tau_j x^j$ and $\sigma_{yy} =
\sum_j \nu_j x^j$, read off the marched state and the flux variables, with $b_j'$
in $F_j$ taken from the second line of \eqref{eq:firstorder2d}, so
nothing is differentiated numerically. As in the jump construction
(Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils}), the constants in $u$ and $v$ have no
stress and the shear pair $(y, 0)$, $(0, x)$ share theirs, through any
edge and not only for constant coefficients, so the same three columns
are dropped: $27$ stress seeds from the $30$ velocity seeds, and the
$20$ velocity seeds of degree $\le 3$ for the velocity block, the
dissertation's counts.

\para{Anchor, scaling and jets}
The seeds are anchored at the evaluation node in both coordinates and
scaled by $r_{\max}$, the distance to the farthest node of the stencil,
so that $S_e \approx X^a Y^b$ in $(X, Y) = (x - x_e, y - y_e) / r_{\max}$
and the material is sampled at $y_e + r_{\max} Y$. The jump basis of
Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils} keeps its origin at the interface point.
Moving the origin in $x$ is a change of basis within the same span and
changes no weight, and the two conventions are reconciled that way, so
the jump path is untouched. Where the \emph{normal} coordinate is
anchored is not a change of basis, because that is where the jet is
imposed and the material is frozen in $C$; there the seeds follow the
lesson of one dimension, that anchoring at the evaluation point is what
keeps the interpolation block conditioned. At the anchor the velocity
seeds have the monomials' first derivatives (only $X$ and $Y$ have one),
and the derivatives of the stress seeds come from the state and the
right-hand side of \eqref{eq:firstorder2d} at $Y = 0$, so the elastic
rates of every basis function at the evaluation node are known exactly.
One of the implementation's tests is the identity this implies: the
velocity rate of a stress seed at the anchor, $(\partial_X \sigma_{xx} +
\partial_Y \sigma_{xy}) / \rho_e$, is the constant term of the seed's
column of $C$.

\para{Checks}
Three properties were checked on a 19-node stencil of a $400$-node set
($h = 0.05$) on the row nearest the lower interface, the anchor $h / 2$
into the band or $h / 2$ into the background. \emph{Jump limit}: as
$\delta \to 0$ the state passes through the edge unchanged, which is
velocity and traction continuity, and on the far side the chain holds
with the anchor's frozen coefficients, which is what the continuity
matrices encode; so the seeds should tend to the translated basis of the
jump case re-expanded about the anchor, and they do, at first order in
$\delta$: the largest difference relative to the largest basis value is
$1.0$ to $2.3\%$ at $\delta = 10^{-3}$ and falls tenfold per decade to
$1.0$ to $2.4 \times 10^{-4}$ at $\delta = 10^{-5}$, for the velocity and
the stress basis and with the anchor on either side. \emph{Residual}:
every seed, evaluated as a function of $(x, y)$ on a $25 \times 401$
tensor grid through a $\delta = 0.01$ edge and pushed through the
elastic operator by eighth-order finite differences in both variables,
satisfies \eqref{eq:chain2d} with relative residual $2 \times 10^{-12}$;
the test uses none of the module's coefficient bookkeeping, and the
residual rises to $0.23$ if the factor $(j + 2)(j + 1)$ in
\eqref{eq:firstorder2d} is wrong and to $0.06$ if the $\lambda (j + 1)
b_{j+1}'$ term is dropped. \emph{Conditioning}: the 2-norm condition
numbers of the velocity block ($38 \times 20$: two components at $19$
nodes against $20$ seeds) and the stress block ($57 \times 27$) are $17$
to $34$ and $24$ to $30$ for $\delta$ from $h$ down to $h / 5000$,
against $34$ and $30$ for the polynomial jump basis re-expanded about the
same anchor, and they tend to those values as $\delta \to 0$. No
Chebyshev-system argument is available in two dimensions, where
interpolation on scattered nodes can be singular even for polynomials.
The saddle-point solve is nonsingular when the Gaussian block is
positive definite, which it is, and the augmentation has full column
rank; for the seeds the measured conditioning is the evidence for the
latter, and it is all we have.

\subsection{Stencil assembly}
\label{sec:assembly2d}
\para{Which rows}
The seed scheme rebuilds the rows of every node whose $19$ nearest
nodes see different values of $\lambda$, $\mu$ or $\rho$, the 1-D rule
of Section~\ref{sec:assembly1d} on the stencil's footprint: with exact
inequality that reaches about $19\delta$ from each edge centre plus the
stencil's radius, and a relative tolerance on the spread trims the tails
(the trim of Section~\ref{sec:results2d}). The nearest interface, by
vertical offset, sets the frame of a rebuilt row; the material profile
along its normal carries both edges of the band, so a stencil that sees
both is marched through both, and the triple stencils of the jump case
are not needed.

\para{Elastic rows}
The rebuilt elastic rows are the coupled saddle-point solves of
Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils} with the seeds in place of the piecewise
polynomials: the Gaussian block on the $19$ nodes is unchanged, the
augmentation is the $38 \times 20$ block of velocity-seed values and the
$57 \times 27$ block of stress-seed values, the right-hand sides on the
seed columns are the elastic rates of \eqref{eq:sys2d} at the evaluation
node, from the jets, and the material in those rates is the anchor
material, which is the node's own. The weights are rotated back to the
laboratory frame as before. Every stencil's seeds are its own, even on a
flat edge, because the jet and the frozen $C$ live at its anchor.

\para{Hyperviscosity rows}
The $\Delta^3$ rows of the same nodes are built on the naive footprint
of $30$ nodes, with the same seeds annihilated: the saddle-point solve
imposes $\Delta^3 S_e = 0$ on every seed column, as it imposes $\Delta^3
p = 0$ on the polynomial augmentation of a standard stencil. That is a
second march per stencil, over the larger radius. Two things decided the
footprint, both measured in Section~\ref{sec:results2d}. A $19$-node
stencil carrying $20$ coupled constraints has $18$ degrees of freedom
left for the Gaussian part of a sixth-order operator, and what comes out
is barely a $\Delta^3$: on the $900$-node set the extreme eigenvalue of
its $\Delta^3$ block is $-82$ against $-151$ for the standard rows, half
the damping, and the operator built that way is unstable at the standard
$\gamma$ once the seeded region, $19\delta$ plus the stencil radius, has
grown to take in every row, from about $\delta = h / 2$ on that node
set; and the alternative
of $30$-node, degree-four stencils for the elastic rows themselves is
stable through a resolved edge but violently unstable through a sharp
one, as a degree-four basis across a jump is, which is presumably why
the original work settled on $19$ nodes and degree three. Annihilating
the seed space is a choice, not a derivation: the seeds' true sixth
derivatives at the anchor are not zero in a varying medium, and using
them as right-hand sides is the alternative Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
records as future work.

\para{Checks}
Without contrast the seed weights equal the degree-three RBF-FD weights
and the no-contrast weights of the jump path to $10^{-10}$; as
$\delta \to 0$ the elastic rows tend to the interface-aware weights at
first order in $\delta$ on a real stencil, all four blocks, whichever side
carries the standard monomials (the $\Delta^3$ rows do not, being built
on $30$ nodes where the jump path's are on $19$, so the seed operator's
$\delta \to 0$ limit differs from the jump-aware operator in its
hyperviscosity rows alone); every block is exact on the seed space through a
$\delta = h / 4$ edge and the $\Delta^3$ blocks annihilate it; and the
rebuilt rows of the assembled operator are exactly the stencils that
see the edge, with no other entry moved. The jump path of
Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils} is unchanged bit for bit.

\para{An ablation}
To measure what the tangential structure of the seeds contributes,
Section~\ref{sec:results2d} uses a variant in which only the seeds of
the pure normal monomials $y^b$ are marched through the edge and every
$x^a y^b$ with $a \ge 1$ is the plain monomial with the anchor
material's stress. The plain columns come from a second chain on the
material frozen at the anchor, since a frozen column must see frozen
lower seeds on its right-hand side or $x^2 y^2$ would still be driven by
the marched $y^2$. The span has the same dimension and the same jets.

\para{Cost}
A 19-node march costs $21$ to $25$\,ms and a seed row, with its 30-node
march, $53$\,ms, in the reference implementation. On $10{,}000$ nodes
the operator through $\delta = h / 8$ has $1965$ seed rows and builds in
$105$\,s; through $\delta = 0.01 = h$ it has $6719$ and builds in
$331$\,s. The marches run on a process pool and the assembled operators
are cached, which is what the sweeps of Section~\ref{sec:results2d}
needed. Two optimisations were not built: the 19-node stencil is a
prefix of the 30-node one, so one march with the seeds rescaled to the
smaller $r_{\max}$ would serve both rows; and on a flat edge every
stencil of one fixed row shares $y_e$ and the profile, so one march with
dense output could serve a whole row.

\subsection{Curved edges}
\label{sec:curved2d}
For a curved edge the material no longer depends on the normal
coordinate of one frame, and three routes were open at the design stage:
(a) march the straight-edge seeds along the true normal through the
stencil's foot point and treat the curvature as a perturbation, $\kappa h
\ll 1$ over a stencil; (b) solve \eqref{eq:chain2d} as a small
boundary-value problem on a patch around each stencil, on a fine local
grid with the harmonic polynomial as boundary data, the oversampling
construction of multiscale finite elements \cite{HouWu1997} and the
honest ``one PDE per stencil''; or (c) compute harmonic coordinates once
globally \cite{OwhadiZhang2007} and take polynomials in them as the
degree-one seeds. Route (a) is what was built, at its zeroth order.

\para{The medium}
With a curved interface the blend of \eqref{eq:profile2d} is taken in
the true signed normal distance: the foot point comes from the same
fixed-point iteration that places the stencil frames, and the distance
is the displacement's component along the normal there, so the foot
point's error enters squared and the distance is exact to rounding
wherever the iteration converges at all. The two edges of the band are
read on one periodic image of it, the image whose centre line is
nearest; reading each edge on its own nearest image pairs the lower edge
of one band with the upper edge of the next around $y = 0.75$, and was
how the first version failed its own test.

\para{The seeds}
The profile of a rebuilt row carries the tangent angle $\theta$ at its
foot point $(x_0, y_0)$ and samples the medium along the true normal,
$(x_0 - y \sin\theta,\, y_0 + y \cos\theta)$, with the march's stops at
the crossings of that line with each interface and its images, by
Newton's method from the vertical estimate. Along that line the blend
is exactly the medium's $\tanh$ profile in $y$ for the interface the
line is normal to (to $10^{-13}$), so the seeds of a curved-edge
stencil are the \emph{flat} seeds of the same local coordinates (to
$10^{-12}$ through a $\delta = 0.005$ edge on a stencil tilted by $0.08$
radians): the ansatz extends the normal profile unchanged along the
tangent. That is the locally flat approximation the jump stencils of
Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils} make, zeroth order in the curvature, and
nothing else in the construction changes: the frames, the anchor at the
evaluation node, the $r_{\max}$ scaling, the 19-node elastic rows and
the 30-node $\Delta^3$ rows are those of Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d}.
A curved march costs $3.6\times$ a flat one, $131$ against $37$\,ms per
19-node stencil measured side by side while the reference study had the
other cores (Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d} quotes $21$ to $25$\,ms for a
flat march on its own), because every material sample along the normal is a
blend evaluation with two foot-point iterations and the march takes a
few thousand of them.

\para{What route (a) gets wrong}
In the tangent frame a node at tangential offset $x$ from the foot
point lies at true normal distance $y - \kappa x^2 / 2$ from the curve up
to terms cubic in the offsets ($\kappa' x^3 / 6$, $\kappa x^2 y / 2$ and
smaller), while its seed value assumes $y$; $\kappa x^2 / 2$ is the
leading sagitta. Over a stencil radius
$r \approx 2.5h$ that is $\kappa r^2 / 2$: for the sine interfaces of
amplitude $0.02$, whose curvature reaches $\kappa = 0.02\,(2\pi)^2 =
0.79$, it is $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ at $2500$ nodes and $1.3 \times 10^{-4}$
at $19{,}600$, to be compared with $\delta$: a tenth of a $\delta = 0.01$
edge at the coarse end and $5\%$ of a $\delta = 0.0025$ edge at the fine
end. So the geometric error of route (a) is largest where the seeds
matter most, through the sharpest edge, and it shrinks with $n$ only as
$h^2$. Section~\ref{sec:results2d} measures what it costs on the node
sets we can afford, and finds route (a) at the seed operator's own floor
through $\delta = 0.005$ and $0.01$ and within $1.5\times$ of it through
$\delta = 0.0025$, so neither (b) nor (c) was built. If a sharper edge or a
tighter curve ever asks for more, the cheaper first step is not the
boundary-value problem but the curvilinear evaluation of the same
marched seeds: evaluate each seed at the node's true arclength and
normal distance instead of its tangent coordinates, and scale the
tangential jets at the anchor by $1 / (1 - \kappa y_e)$. That makes the
material exact at every node at no march cost and leaves only the
operator's curvature terms as the error; Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
lists it first among the things to do next.

This section is the two-dimensional test of the construction of
Section~\ref{sec:seeds2d}, on the flat and curved geometries of
Section~\ref{sec:setting2d}. It answers three questions in turn. Whether
the seed-augmented operator is stable at the hyperviscosity the standard
scheme was tuned for (Section~\ref{sec:stability2d}); what the seeds do
through a flat edge at normal incidence, which settles the rule of when
to seed (Section~\ref{sec:flatsweep}); and whether the same holds at
oblique incidence, where the tangential structure of the seeds is put to
work (Section~\ref{sec:oblique2d}), and through a curved edge, where the
seeds are marched along the true normal (Section~\ref{sec:curvedresults}).
Section~\ref{sec:tests2d} describes the test problems and the reference
solutions, and Section~\ref{sec:naive2d} what the standard scheme does on
its own, which is what the seeds have to improve on.

\subsection{Test problems and references}
\label{sec:tests2d}
\para{Pulses and floors}
Every run in this section uses the medium, node sets, stencils,
hyperviscosity and time step of Section~\ref{sec:setting2d}, the node
counts $n = 2500$, $4900$, $10{,}000$ and $19{,}600$ ($h$ from $0.02$ to
$0.0071$), and $t = 1$ as the measurement time. The pulse at normal
incidence is the Gaussian plane P-wave of sharpness $15$ started at
$y = 0.875$, whose tails are $2 \times 10^{-14}$ at the edges. We tried
the dissertation's narrower pulse first (sharpness $23$ from $y = 0.75$)
and found its resolution floor, $10^{-2}$ in $v$ on the finest node set
against $10^{-7}$ in the one-dimensional study, high enough to hide
everything but the jump; the wider pulse lowers the floor by $8\times$ at
$19{,}600$ nodes, and the pulse can be widened no further if its tails
are to clear both edges. Two floors are used. The
\emph{resolution floor} is the same pulse on the same nodes in the
uniform background, measured against its exact translate: the error of
the standard scheme with no edge at all. The \emph{seed floor} is the
seed operator's own: the same rows that a given $\delta$ would seed,
built through a contrast of $10^{-6}$, a medium that differs from
uniform by $10^{-6}$ of the pulse, three orders below any error here, and
through which the seeds equal the monomials to rounding, measured against
the same translate. It exists
because the seeded rows are a different scheme from the bulk, $19$-node
degree-three elastic rows with $30$-node annihilating $\Delta^3$ rows
against $30$-node degree-four stencils, with a resolution floor of their
own.

\para{The two operators}
Both operators are run at every $(n, \delta)$: the standard scheme, which
samples the coefficients at the nodes, and the seed operator of
Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d}, which at $\delta = 0$ is the interface-aware
operator of Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils} and for $\delta > 0$ puts the
seed stencils on every row whose $19$-node stencil sees the edge, tails
included. No rule about which edges to seed was imposed beforehand; the
flat sweep is what sets the rule. On the flat geometry the seeded rows
number $985$, $1627$, $2735$ and $4888$ at $\delta = 0.0025$ ($39\%$ down
to $25\%$ of the nodes, the $19\delta$ tails plus a stencil radius),
$1796$, $3388$, $6719$ and $12{,}929$ at $\delta = 0.01$ ($72\%$ to
$66\%$), and every row at $\delta = 0.04$. Rates are measured in
$h = n^{-1/2}$ between successive node sets. The tables give two
significant digits, three when the leading digit is $1$; the ratios
quoted in the text are computed from the unrounded errors.

\para{References}
Each experiment with $\delta > 0$ has a reference that shares nothing
with the stencils under test; the two jump cases without an exact
solution, the oblique train and the curved interface, use a finer run of
the interface-aware scheme itself, as stated where they occur. For a jump on a flat interface it is the exact solution, the
ray sum of Section~\ref{sec:results1d} applied to the P wave. For a flat
edge of width $\delta > 0$ at normal incidence the solution depends on
$y$ alone, and the reference is the one-dimensional pseudo-spectral
solver of Section~\ref{sec:results1d} on the image of the problem under
$x = 1 - 2y$ (wave speed $2 c_p$, density $\rho / 2$, impedances
unchanged), fed the exact image of the two-dimensional initial state,
with $\sigma_{xx}$ recovered exactly from $\sigma_{yy}$ through
$\partial_t \sigma_{xx} = \lambda v_y$. The two-dimensional blend of
\eqref{eq:profile2d}, linear in the weight for $\lambda$, $\mu$ and
$\rho$, is mapped pointwise rather than rebuilt as a one-dimensional
layer, since the one-dimensional profile \eqref{eq:profile1d} blends $c$
and $\rho$ and the two agree only in the jump limit. This reference tends
to the ray sum at first order in $\delta$ (the largest difference in $v$
at $t = 0.3$ is $0.167$, $0.090$, $0.046$, $0.023$ and $0.012$ for
$\delta = 0.016$ down to $0.001$), doubling its grid or halving its step
moves $v$, $\sigma_{xx}$ and $\sigma_{yy}$ by under $3 \times 10^{-10}$ at
$t = 1$ ($\delta = 0.005$), and it is run on $4096$ nodes for $\delta = 0.0025$ and $1024$
otherwise with $\Delta t = 5 \times 10^{-5}$. Because it depends on $y$
only it is evaluated at every node directly, and no resampling enters
the flat errors.

At oblique incidence on a flat medium the coefficients still depend on
$y$ only, so the Fourier modes of the state in $x$ never mix: each mode
is a one-dimensional system in $y$ with $\px \to 2\pi i k$ and complex
coefficients, Fourier pseudo-spectral in $y$, and all modes march
together under RK4. The reference is exact in $x$ ($13$ modes carry the
train of Section~\ref{sec:oblique2d} to $10^{-14}$), uses $4096$ points
in $y$ for $\delta = 0.0025$, and costs minutes ($220$\,s at
$\delta = 0.0025$, $40$\,s at $0.01$). Its checks: the train in a
uniform medium is its exact translate to $10^{-10}$ and has zero curl;
at normal incidence the solver agrees with the mapped one-dimensional
reference to $10^{-10}$, two independent code paths; through an edge at
oblique incidence the elastic energy is conserved to $10^{-12}$, and
doubling the grid while halving the step moves the answer by $10^{-8}$.
The fields and their derivatives are evaluated at the nodes by
trigonometric interpolation in both directions.

For a curved edge of width $\delta > 0$ the modes mix, and the reference
is the same solver on an $n_x \times n_y$ product grid: derivatives by
FFT along each axis, the material multiplied in physical space, and
RK4 with the step $\Delta t = 0.5 / (c_{p,\max} \sqrt{n_x^2 + n_y^2})$,
the CFL number $0.5$ on the grid's largest wavenumber. The grids are
$512 \times 1024$ for $\delta = 0.01$, $1024 \times 2048$ for $0.005$ and
$2048 \times 4096$ for $0.0025$. On a flat medium at oblique incidence
the product-grid solver agrees with the Fourier-in-$x$ one to
$10^{-11}$, two representations of one scheme; on a curved edge the
energy is conserved to $10^{-9}$, and the curl is no longer zero,
because a curved edge converts P to S. Its error bars, measured at $t = 1$
on $6000$ random points: at $\delta = 0.01$ the $512 \times 1024$ grid
moves by $1.3 \times 10^{-9}$ when $n_x$ is halved and by
$3.1 \times 10^{-9}$ when both directions are doubled or the step is
halved (the same number, because the doubled grid's difference is its
halved step), and at $\delta = 0.005$ the $1024 \times 2048$ grid moves by
$8 \times 10^{-11}$ and $2.6 \times 10^{-10}$ under doubling in $x$ and in
$y$; both are five orders below anything measured. A jump on a curved
interface has no product-grid reference, and for it we use the
interface-aware operator of Section~\ref{sec:jumpstencils} on a node set
of its own, $122{,}500$ nodes ($h = 1 / 350$), resampled onto the
sweep's nodes by one-sided interpolation that never crosses an
interface. Taking the difference between its two resolutions as the bar, the bar is
about $5\%$ of the finest point: runs of $62{,}500$ and $122{,}500$ nodes differ by
$1.7 \times 10^{-4}$ in $v$ on the $19{,}600$-node set, where the
$19{,}600$-node interface-aware run's error is $1.39 \times 10^{-3}$
against the finer reference and $1.25 \times 10^{-3}$ against the
coarser, a reference made by the same scheme flattering a run of it by
the correlation of their errors; the finer one's numbers are the ones
reported. The references cost $201$\,s ($512 \times 1024$), $1952$\,s
($1024 \times 2048$), $3.6$\,h ($2048 \times 4096$) and $286$\,s (the
jump run) to $t = 1$, and are cached.

\para{Errors}
As fixed in Section~\ref{sec:setting2d}: relative $\ell^2$ errors in $v$
and in $\sigma_{yy}$ at $t = 1$ over all nodes, and for $u$ the largest
$|u|$ at normal incidence on a flat edge, where the true $u$ is
identically zero, and the relative $\ell^2$ error in $u$ at oblique
incidence and on the curved geometry, where it is not (a curved edge
makes $u$ of size $0.12$ out of the incident pulse at normal incidence,
and its relative error then measures the scattered field alone).

\subsection{Naive baseline}
\label{sec:naive2d}
The naive rows of Tables~\ref{tab:flat2d_v} and~\ref{tab:flat2d_u} are
the standard scheme through flat edges of width $\delta = 0$, $0.0025$,
$0.01$ and $0.04$ at normal incidence, and the floor rows are the same
pulse with no edge. In the ratio $h / \delta$ the three widths run from
$8$ to $2.9$ (never resolved), from $2$ to $0.71$ (resolved from
$10{,}000$ nodes on) and from $0.5$ to $0.18$ (resolved on every node
set), the three regimes of Section~\ref{sec:results1d}. Four things are
to be read off before the seeds enter.

The resolution floor is the first. Through the edge every node set
resolves, $\delta = 0.04$, the naive error in $v$ is the floor to within
$7\%$ at every $n$, with the floor's rates: a resolved edge costs the
standard scheme nothing. Through the edge no node set resolves,
$\delta = 0.0025$, the naive scheme behaves like the jump: second order
in $v$ (rates $1.9$, $1.6$, $2.2$), $10\times$ the floor at $19{,}600$
nodes, and at $0.6$ to $0.76$ of the jump's error, since the rows that
straddle the edge centre at $h / 2$ see $\tanh(h / 2\delta)$ of the
contrast, $0.9993$ at $2500$ nodes and $0.89$ at $19{,}600$. The knee is
there, at $\delta = 0.01$, but small in $v$: the naive error exceeds the
floor by $19\%$, $17\%$, $18\%$ and $7\%$ from $h = 2\delta$ to
$h = 0.71\delta$, so that the edge's own contribution, taking the excess over
the floor in quadrature as a proxy for it, falls from $3.3 \times 10^{-2}$ to $5.1 \times 10^{-4}$,
faster than second order and accelerating, which is the knee shape of
Section~\ref{sec:results1d} seen through a high floor. And the spurious
$u$ separates the cases an order of magnitude more sharply than $v$
does. The naive stencils excite $u$ where a field varies sharply in $y$,
because $\partial_x$ on scattered nodes does not annihilate such a field
exactly, and $u$ carries none of the pulse's own dispersion error, so
its floor is low ($8.9 \times 10^{-4}$ down to $3.0 \times 10^{-5}$).
Against that floor the jump is $11\times$, $24\times$, $46\times$ and
$194\times$ from $2500$ to $19{,}600$ nodes and $\delta = 0.0025$ is
$11\times$, $24\times$, $39\times$ and $116\times$: an unresolved edge's
error decays like the jump's, slower than the floor, and the ratio grows
with $n$. The resolved edge sits at the floor, $0.8$ to $1.3\times$ it,
at every $n$. The edge of
width $0.01$ sits $3\times$, $3\times$, $6\times$ and $3\times$ above it,
rising while $h \ge \delta$ and falling back once $h < \delta$, a drop of
$9\times$ between $10{,}000$ and $19{,}600$ nodes against $4\times$ for
the floor: the knee of Section~\ref{sec:results1d}, at $h = \delta$ as
there, in the one quantity the floor does not pollute.

Two remarks. The two-dimensional naive scheme is not first order through
an unresolved edge as one-dimensional FD4 was: the fixed rows straddling
the edge centre keep the coefficient sampling symmetric, and RBF-FD
through a jump is already second order with a large constant
\cite{MartinFornberg2017}, so the knee is a change of constant rather
than of order and shows as a bend rather than a kink. And the seeds can
at most remove the excess over the floor: a factor $10$ in $v$ and up to
$116$ in spurious $u$ through $\delta = 0.0025$, $8$ to $19\%$ in $v$
and $3$ to $6\times$ in $u$ through $\delta = 0.01$, nothing through
$\delta = 0.04$. So the $\delta = 0.0025$ column and the spurious $u$
are the primary evidence of the flat sweep, and a knee plot in $v$ alone
would show little.

\subsection{Stability}
\label{sec:stability2d}
The empirical stability recipe for interface-aware RBF-FD, $\Delta^3$
hyperviscosity at $\gamma = 2.4 \times 10^{-11} (h / 0.02)^5$ with the
fixed rows straddling each interface, was found empirically
\cite{Martin2016,MartinFornberg2017}, and the question of this section
is whether it survives an augmentation that is no longer polynomial.
It was measured on the $900$-node flat set ($4500$ eigenvalues, dense) at
the standard $\gamma$ and CFL $0.5$, by the largest real part of the
spectrum with the hyperviscosity in and by the largest RK4 amplification
$\max |R(\lambda \Delta t)|$ over it, $R$ the stability polynomial of RK4
and $\lambda$ an eigenvalue here, not the Lam\'e parameter; $1.0004$ is the naive scheme's own
value on that node set, a mode growing by $4 \times 10^{-4}$ per step and
harmless over a run, and anything above $1.001$ is a run that visibly
grows. Table~\ref{tab:variants900} collects the operators tried.

\para{What fails}
The first construction gave each $19$-node seed row its own $\Delta^3$
row on the same $19$ nodes with the seeds annihilated, the exact twin of
the jump path. It is fine while the seed rows form a band around each
edge ($+6.2 \times 10^{-2}$ at $\delta = h / 8$, $+8.7 \times 10^{-2}$ at
$h / 4$, the naive scheme's level) and unstable once the tails, $19\delta$
plus the stencil radius, make every row a seed row, from about
$\delta = h / 2$ on this node set: $+1.05$ at $h / 2$ and $+2.65$ at
$h$, with $\max |R|$ of $1.003$ and $1.018$. The naive spectrum does not
move with $\delta$, so the movement is the seed rows', but it is not the
seeds. Plain $19$-node degree-three stencils everywhere on the jump
medium, with no seeds at all, give $+2.4$ and worse: the $19$-node
degree-three scheme is unstable at the $\gamma$ tuned for the $30$-node
degree-four one, and the jump path never sees this because its band of
rebuilt rows is $4h$ wide. The reason is at the damping end of the spectrum: the extreme eigenvalue of the $\Delta^3$ block of the all-seed
operator is $-82$ against $-151$ for the naive one, half the damping,
because a $19$-node stencil carrying $20$ coupled constraints leaves $18$
degrees of freedom for the Gaussian part of a sixth-order operator and
what comes out is barely a $\Delta^3$. Trimming the seed rows with a
tolerance on the material spread does not help (at $\delta = h / 2$,
$574$, $458$ and $420$ rows in place of $900$ leave $+1.05$, $+1.05$ and
$+0.35$): it is the width of the contiguous $19$-node region that
matters, not the count. Doubling $\gamma$, everywhere or on the seed rows
alone, stabilises every width ($+2.9 \times 10^{-2}$ at $h / 2$,
$+3.9 \times 10^{-2}$ at $h$, and $1.5\gamma$ does as well) at the price
of the extra damping. Building the seed rows themselves on the naive
footprint, $30$ nodes and degree four, is stable through a resolved edge
($+0.10$, $+0.05$, $+0.06$ at $\delta = h / 2$, $h$, $1.9h$) and
violently unstable through a sharp one ($+30$ and $+28$ at $h / 8$ and
$h / 4$, $\max |R|$ up to $1.23$), exactly as the jump path is with
degree-four stencils across the interface ($+17$, $\max |R|$ $1.12$):
a degree-four basis across a sharp feature is the problem, presumably
why the original work settled on $19$ nodes and degree three, and
conditioning is not the cause (the $30$-node seed blocks are at $85$ to
$215$). Keeping the naive $\Delta^3$ rows on the seed rows is stable
everywhere, but the naive $\Delta^3$ does not annihilate the seeds, and
the seeds are what the scheme resolves through the edge.

\para{What works}
The elastic rows keep the $19$-node seed stencils, and the $\Delta^3$
rows of the same nodes are built on the naive $30$-node footprint with
the same seeds annihilated, the construction of
Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d}. It is stable by this criterion at every width at the
standard $\gamma$, $+4.1 \times 10^{-2}$ to $+6.6 \times 10^{-2}$ with
$\max |R| \le 1.0005$, and the extreme $\Delta^3$ eigenvalue is back at
$-176$: the naive damping restored on the seed rows. As a check that
the choice is not bought with accuracy, the variants were run on
$2500$ nodes through $\delta = h / 8$ with the pulse of
Section~\ref{sec:tests2d}: every seed variant removes most of the naive
excess (spurious $u$ down $4$ to $9\times$, to $1$ to $2.5\times$ the
floor, and $v$ to the floor), the chosen one is the best in $v$
($3.0 \times 10^{-2}$ against $4.4 \times 10^{-2}$ with the naive
$\Delta^3$ rows kept and $4.7 \times 10^{-2}$ with $19$-node rows, below
the naive floor of $5.2 \times 10^{-2}$) and keeps the most energy
($E(1) / E(0) = 0.995$), and the $2\gamma$ variant buys the smallest $u$
with twice the chosen operator's error in $v$. The answer to the
question is therefore yes: RBF-FD stability survives seed-augmented
stencils at the standard $\gamma$ and with the straddling rows kept,
provided the hyperviscosity row of a seed stencil has the footprint
$\gamma$ was tuned for. Annihilating the seed space was the only variant
tried that both annihilates and is stable, and the alternative of
Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d}, the seeds' true sixth derivatives as
right-hand sides, was never needed.

\para{An edge the nodes never resolve}
Through $\delta = 0.0025$, $h / \delta$ from $8$ to $2.9$, the seeds are

$3.6$, $3.2$, $3.5$ in $\sigma_{yy}$ against $1.6$ to $2.2$ for the naive
scheme, which is $2.9\times$, $6.2\times$, $13\times$ and $20\times$
worse in $v$ and $5.5\times$, $16\times$, $25\times$ and $67\times$ worse
in spurious $u$ from $2500$ to $19{,}600$ nodes; the seeds' $u$ sits at
$1.5$ to $1.9\times$ the floor's, where the naive scheme's sits at
$11$ to $116\times$. That is the one-dimensional result in two
dimensions: the order of the jump-aware stencils, reached without the
nodes seeing the edge, on the same nodes and the same time step. The
constant is not the jump row's but about half of it at every $n$
($3.0 \times 10^{-2}$ against $5.4 \times 10^{-2}$ at $2500$ nodes,
$6.4 \times 10^{-4}$ against $1.23 \times 10^{-3}$ at $19{,}600$), and
half the uniform-medium floor's. That is the seed operator's own floor,
not the edge: seeding the same rows through a contrast of $10^{-6}$
gives $2.7 \times 10^{-2}$, $6.2 \times 10^{-3}$, $1.9 \times 10^{-3}$
and $6.9 \times 10^{-4}$, half the naive floor at every $n$ and within
$10$ to $25\%$ of the seed errors through $\delta = 0.0025$. So the seeds
remove the edge error down to their own resolution floor, as the
jump-aware operator removes it down to the naive one. Why a band of
$19$-node degree-three seed rows with $30$-node annihilating $\Delta^3$
rows is more accurate on this pulse than $30$-node degree-four stencils
everywhere is not settled. It is not the degree, since plain $19$-node
degree-three stencils everywhere with their own $\Delta^3$ rows are $2$
to $4\times$ worse than the standard scheme ($9.5 \times 10^{-2}$ down to
$4.9 \times 10^{-3}$; the operator Section~\ref{sec:stability2d} found
unstable at the standard $\gamma$, harmless to $t = 1$), and it is not
less damping, since the energy ratios match the floor's ($0.93$, $0.92$,
$0.99$, $0.99$ for both). The variants of Section~\ref{sec:stability2d}
point at the $\Delta^3$ rows, and a dispersion study of those rows in a
uniform medium would settle it; nothing in what follows depends on it.

\para{The crossover}
Through $\delta = 0.01$ the seeds lead by $2.1\times$ in $v$ at
$h = 2\delta$ (trailing by $1.3\times$ in $u$), $2.0\times$ at $h = 1.4\delta$ ($u$
$1.4\times$) and $1.5\times$ at $h = \delta$ ($u$ $1.8\times$), and trail
by $0.88\times$ ($u$ $0.75\times$) at $h = 0.71\delta$. Across the panel
the seed rates fall from $3.1$ to $2.7$ while the naive rates rise from
$3.1$ to $4.2$: the naive scheme is resolving the edge, and the seeds are
seeding rows that no longer need it, $66$ to $72\%$ of the nodes here.
Through the edge every node set resolves, $\delta = 0.04$, where every
row is a seed row, the seeds are $1.4$ to $2.0\times$ worse than the
naive scheme in $v$ and $3.7$ to $5.7\times$ worse in $u$, at rates of
$2.9$ to $3.6$ against $2.9$ to $3.9$, as the $2500$-node acceptance run
already found. The naive scheme is at its floor to within $7\%$ at every
$n$; there is nothing left for the seeds to remove, and the $19$-node
degree-three stencils through a resolved profile are the less accurate
scheme. The break-even is between $h = \delta$ and $h = 0.71\delta$, and
the estimate $\delta \lesssim h / 4$ that the three points of
Section~\ref{sec:stability2d} suggested was too cautious: at
$\delta = h / 2$ the seeds halve $v$ and only just lose in $u$.

\para{The rule}
\emph{Seed when $\delta \le h$, and run the standard scheme otherwise.}
The rule is per edge, $\delta$ the edge's width and $h$ the node set's
spacing, not per row: the tolerance on the material spread of
Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d} trims the tails of the seeded region, and
the accuracy lost on a resolved edge comes from the rows at its centre,
not its tails. With the rule the seed operator is never worse than the
naive scheme in $v$, the error the rule was set on, and is at or below
the resolution floor wherever it is used (in spurious $u$ it trails by
$1.3\times$ at the one seeded point $h = 2\delta$, $\delta = 0.01$); in this sweep it means every node set at $\delta = 0.0025$, up to
$10{,}000$ nodes at $\delta = 0.01$ and never at $\delta = 0.04$. The
comparison is against coefficient sampling on the same nodes; the
coefficient treatments of seismic finite differences were run on this
operator too (below) and on the one-dimensional scheme
(Section~\ref{sec:results1d}), and none of them reaches the seeds' order.
In one dimension the rule is safe and conservative
(Section~\ref{sec:assembly1d}): seeding never costs accuracy there, the
seeds still lead by $120\times$ at $h = \delta / 2$ through
$\delta = 0.0025$ in Table~\ref{tab:knee1d}, and on the resolved edge
$\delta = 0.04$ the two schemes agree to within $1\%$, so nothing is
gained by seeding it.

\subsection{Oblique incidence}
\label{sec:oblique2d}
At normal incidence on a flat edge the solution is independent of $x$
and $u \equiv 0$, so the seeds of the monomials $x^a y^b$ with $a \ge 1$
can show only through the weights, never through the solution. This
experiment sends the wave in at an angle, where $u \ne 0$, the tangential
strain of the incident P wave meets the edge, and P-to-S conversion
appears.

\para{The train}
A single tilted plane pulse cannot be periodic on the unit square and
stay out of the band, so the initial data is the doubly periodic train
of Section~\ref{sec:setting2d}: crests $x - 2y = \mathrm{const}$, at
$26.6^\circ$ to the edge normal, travelling towards $+x$ and $-y$, one
crest through $(0, 0.875)$ repeated every $0.447$ along the direction of
travel with the Gaussian profile of sharpness $15$, so that neighbouring
crests overlap at $1.3 \times 10^{-5}$ and $13$ Fourier modes in $x$
carry the train to $10^{-14}$. The direction $(1, 2)$ has the largest
crest spacing of any lattice direction below the critical angle (P-to-P
transmission into the band turns critical at $45^\circ$); at $26.6^\circ$
the transmitted P, transmitted S and reflected S waves leave at
$39^\circ$, $21^\circ$ and $15^\circ$, all propagating. A quarter of each
strip starts inside the band. Every point carries the \emph{background}
material's P eigenvector, so that every field is one smooth profile
everywhere and the part inside the band is a smooth superposition of the
band's own waves. Our first attempt used the local material's
eigenvector, which makes the tractions jump by the impedance ratio,
$2.8\times$, over the width $\delta$ at every edge crossing; the true
dynamics resolve that into $O(1)$ waves with $\delta$-sharp fronts, and
both schemes then sat $50\times$ above the resolution floor at
first-order rates whatever the stencils did at the edge. Initial data
must respect the interface conditions at the sub-grid scale, or every
scheme measures the initial data.

\para{What is measured}
The sweep of Section~\ref{sec:flatsweep} through $\delta = 0.0025$ and
$0.01$ with the same node sets and the same seed operators, which do not
depend on the pulse: relative errors in $v$, $u$ and $\sigma_{yy}$
against the Fourier-in-$x$ reference, the two floors, and the ablation
of Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d}, in which only the seeds of the pure
normal monomials $y^b$ are marched and every $x^a y^b$ with $a \ge 1$ is
the plain monomial. What the ablation removes is, for instance, the
$v$-component that the seed of $u = x$ acquires across the edge ($0.5$
at $\delta = h / 4$ on a $400$-node stencil): continuity of the normal
stress $\lambda u_x + K v_y$ with $\lambda$ changing forces a kink in
$v_y$, the Poisson coupling of the edge, which a monomial cannot carry.

\para{What the seeds do}
Tables~\ref{tab:oblique2d_v}, \ref{tab:oblique2d_u}
and~\ref{tab:oblique2d_syy} and Figure~\ref{fig:obliqueconv} give the
results. The seeds win at every $(n, \delta)$, by less than at normal
incidence: $1.4\times$, $1.7\times$, $1.9\times$ and $1.7\times$ in $v$
and $1.2\times$, $1.3\times$, $1.5\times$ and $1.6\times$ in $u$ through
$\delta = 0.0025$, and $1.4$ to $1.7\times$ in $v$ and $1.1$ to
$1.7\times$ in $u$ through $\delta = 0.01$, where the naive scheme has
$h \le 2\delta$ and is resolving the edge. They still lead there at
$h = 0.71\delta$, by $1.6\times$, so the rule of
Section~\ref{sec:flatsweep} is safe on the seeded side here; it was set
on $v$ at normal incidence, and this train does not reach below
$h = 0.71\delta$. The tangential seeds are what acts, and they act where
the edge is sharp. Through $\delta = 0.0025$ the ablation is worse than
the full seeds by $1.2\times$, $1.6\times$, $2.2\times$ and $2.3\times$
in $v$ and $1.3\times$, $1.7\times$, $2.4\times$ and $2.7\times$ in $u$,
and it is worse than the \emph{naive} scheme in $u$ at every $n$ and in
$v$ from $10{,}000$ nodes on ($1.91 \times 10^{-2}$ against
$1.40 \times 10^{-2}$ in $v$ at $19{,}600$ nodes, $4.3 \times 10^{-2}$
against $2.6 \times 10^{-2}$ in $u$): the normal-only seeds bend $v$
across the edge for the $y^b$ columns and leave $u = x$ straight, an
inconsistent pair that costs more than doing nothing. Through
$\delta = 0.01$ the ablation and the full seeds agree to within $4\%$
at every $n$: with $h \le 2\delta$ the kink of the tangential columns is
spread over half a stencil radius or more, and a cubic in $y$ carries
it. So the same crossover $h \approx \delta$ that decides whether to seed
decides whether the tangential columns are worth their march.

\para{The order, reported as it is}
The order is not four. Through $\delta = 0.0025$ the seeds converge at
$2.5$, $2.3$ and $1.9$ in $v$ (the naive scheme at $2.0$, $2.0$ and $2.2$)
and sit $7\times$ above the train's resolution floor at $19{,}600$
nodes, where at normal incidence they sat at half the floor; in
$\sigma_{yy}$ they are no better than the naive scheme at $19{,}600$
nodes ($1.62 \times 10^{-2}$ against $1.58 \times 10^{-2}$, at rates of
$1.3$ to $1.5$). Six measurements located where this comes from. It is
not the hyperviscosity: at $\gamma / 4$ the seeds' $v$ moves by under
$15\%$ wherever the run stays stable. It is not the angle: the trains
along $(1, 3)$, $(1, 4)$ and $(1, 6)$, at $18.4^\circ$, $14.0^\circ$ and
$9.5^\circ$, give the same rates with a constant that falls with the
angle ($8.3 \times 10^{-3}$, $7.5 \times 10^{-3}$, $6.5 \times 10^{-3}$
and $4.8 \times 10^{-3}$ at $19{,}600$ nodes from $26.6^\circ$ to
$9.5^\circ$). It is not the $u$-component seeds: an S pulse at normal
incidence, which has no $x$-dependence and no conversion, comes through
$\delta = 0.0025$ at rates $4.4$, $3.9$ and $3.2$ in $u$, at half the
naive floor and with a spurious $v$ of $2.7 \times 10^{-5}$ against the
naive scheme's $6.2 \times 10^{-4}$ at $19{,}600$ nodes, the
normal-incidence result in the other polarisation. The jump-aware
stencils do the same: the interface-aware operator at $\delta = 0$ with
this train, measured against a $78{,}400$-node run of itself resampled
one-sided, converges at $2.4$, $2.5$ and $2.4$ in $v$ with
$8.9 \times 10^{-3}$ at $19{,}600$ nodes, and the seeds through
$\delta = 0.0025$ ($8.3 \times 10^{-3}$) are where their $\delta \to 0$
limit is; they keep the order of the stencils they generalise, an order
that was never measured at oblique incidence in
\cite{Martin2016,MartinFornberg2017}. The stencils at the edge are not
the bottleneck: the truncation error of the elastic operator on the
exact reference state at $t = 1$ is fourth order for the bulk rows and
for the seed rows at normal incidence, while at oblique incidence the
\emph{bulk} rows, nowhere near an edge, sit at $5.8 \times 10^{-2}$ down
to $2.2 \times 10^{-2}$, first order, and the seed rows below them. And
the evidence points to the converted S waves: they keep the P pulse's temporal spectrum
at wavelengths shorter by $c_p / c_s = 1.73$ on both sides of each edge,
the spatial profile of an S pulse of sharpness $26$, whose own
resolution floor in the uniform medium is $2.1 \times 10^{-1}$ down to
$1.19 \times 10^{-2}$ at rates $2.0$, $2.8$ and $3.5$: pre-asymptotic on
every node set here, and at $19{,}600$ nodes above the seeds' whole
error. Weighted by the S waves' share of the solution it matches the seeds'
error, rates included; this is the supported explanation, not a proof
that nothing else contributes. So at oblique incidence the seeds keep the order
of the jump-aware stencils and land at the resolution floor of the
mode-converted S waves, which the train's own floor, a P wave, does not
see; the right yardstick for an oblique run is a floor with the
converted content in it, and by that estimate fourth-order behaviour of any scheme on this problem
needs $h$ below about $1 / 300$, the next node set up. We report the
result as it is.

\subsection{The curved edge}
\label{sec:curvedresults}
The last experiment bends both interfaces into the sine curves of
amplitude $0.02$ of Section~\ref{sec:setting2d}, curvature up to
$\kappa = 0.79$ and tilt up to $7.2^\circ$, with edges of width $\delta$
in the true signed normal distance and the seeds marched along the true
normal, route (a) of Section~\ref{sec:curved2d}. Everything is at normal
incidence, which is locally oblique by up to $7.2^\circ$, so the
tangential seeds of Section~\ref{sec:oblique2d} act on every stencil not
at a crest or a trough, while mode conversion at $7^\circ$ is weak and
the converted-S floor of that section should be small. The node sets are
those of Section~\ref{sec:setting2d} with their rows straddling the sine curves, independent of $\delta$; the widths are $\delta = 0$, $0.005$,
$0.01$ and then $0.0025$; the seeded rows number $1320$, $2452$, $4631$
and $8553$ at $\delta = 0.005$ ($53\%$ down to $44\%$ of the nodes) and
match the flat counts to within a percent at $0.0025$ and $0.01$; the
references are the product-grid solver and the $122{,}500$-node
jump-aware run of Section~\ref{sec:tests2d}. Two probes are added. The
error in $u$ is now the relative error against the reference's own $u$,
which the curved edges make out of the incident pulse ($0.12$ at most),
so it measures the scattered field alone. And the \emph{truncation
probe} applies each operator to the reference state at $t = 1$ on the
nodes and compares with the exact rate from the reference's own
derivatives and the material at the nodes, as a relative $\ell^2$ error
over the seeded rows and over the rest, for both operators (the naive
operator's ``edge rows'' are the rows the seeds would rebuild); this is
the direct measurement of what route (a)'s geometry costs the stencils,
with no time stepping in between.

\para{The jump stencils, and the edge the nodes do not resolve}

\para{The seeds' floor}
The seed floor is below the naive floor at every $n$, but by less at the
fine end: $3.8\times$ at $4900$ nodes and $2.9\times$ at $10{,}000$
($4.7 \times 10^{-3}$ against $1.81 \times 10^{-2}$ and
$1.68 \times 10^{-3}$ against $4.8 \times 10^{-3}$ at $\delta = 0.005$),
only $1.6\times$ at $19{,}600$ ($8.3 \times 10^{-4}$ against
$1.30 \times 10^{-3}$), its rates having fallen to $2.1$ to $2.6$ there
against the naive floor's $3.7$ to $3.9$:
$19$-node degree-three rows are third order in the first derivatives
with a small constant, and with exact inequality they cover the
$19\delta$ tails on both sides of both edges, $44$ to $72\%$ of the
nodes. That is the mechanism behind the seeds' rates of $2.4$ to $2.7$
at the fine end, here and in Sections~\ref{sec:flatsweep}
and~\ref{sec:oblique2d}: a resolution effect of the seeded region's
stencils and not of the edge, which the trim below confirms. The
scattered field says the same from the other side. In $u$ the jump
stencils are $4.2\times$ better than the naive scheme at $19{,}600$
nodes ($6.6 \times 10^{-3}$ against $2.8 \times 10^{-2}$) at rates $2.5$,
$3.1$, $3.4$ against $2.0$, $2.2$, $1.6$; through $\delta = 0.005$ the
seeds are $2.3\times$ better ($2.6 \times 10^{-3}$ against
$6.0 \times 10^{-3}$) at $3.4$, $3.5$, $3.7$; through $\delta = 0.01$ the
two agree at $19{,}600$ nodes ($3.9 \times 10^{-3}$ against
$4.4 \times 10^{-3}$), where the naive scheme resolves the edge. The
seeds' $u$ converges faster than their $v$ at $\delta = 0.005$ ($3.4$ to
$3.7$ against $2.3$ to $2.5$) because the $v$ floor at the fine end is
the seeded rows' resolution of the \emph{incident} pulse, which $u$,
born at the edge, does not carry.

\para{The route (b) decision, and cost}
On this evidence route (b) of Section~\ref{sec:curved2d}, the
boundary-value problem per stencil, is not needed on these node sets:
through $\delta = 0.005$ and $0.01$ the route (a) seeds sit at the seed
operator's own floor at every $n$, their rows' truncation error on the
true curved solution converges at the bulk rate, through $\delta = 0.0025$
the geometry first shows as $1.4$ to $1.5\times$ the flat seeds' error
at $10{,}000$ and $19{,}600$ nodes, a contribution level with the seed
floor and half the naive floor, and the curved sweep reproduces the flat
one. The floor route (a) does impose is the geometric one,
$\kappa r^2 / 2$ against $\delta$, and it is below the resolution floor
of these node sets at every width run. What would make (b) necessary is
a radius of curvature comparable to the stencil, or a $\delta$ so small
that $\kappa r^2 / 2\delta$ is of order one at a resolution the node set
can still afford; neither is this geometry, and if either arises the
cheaper first step is the curvilinear evaluation of the same marched
seeds (Sections~\ref{sec:curved2d} and~\ref{sec:discussion}). Two things
were not done: the oblique train on the curved geometry, which the
product-grid reference could take as it takes any initial state, and
the tuning of the seeded width. The costs, for the record: the flat
sweep takes $636$\,s on $12$ workers with the seed operators to build
(the largest, $19{,}600$ nodes at $\delta = 0.04$, in $94$\,s) and about
$4$ minutes once they are cached; the oblique sweep $500$\,s with the
operators cached, $260$\,s of it the two references; on the curved
geometry the references took $45$ minutes plus $3.6$ hours for the
$2048 \times 4096$ grid, the $24$ seed operators about an hour on $6$ to
$12$ workers (the two $19{,}600$-node operators $330$ and $475$\,s on
six), the sweep $12$ minutes from the caches and the spectra $23$
minutes. A curved march costs $3.6\times$ a flat one
(Section~\ref{sec:curved2d}), and none of this is tuned.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Section~\ref{sec:scope} lists what the implementation leaves out of the
2016--2017 methods. The restrictions below are those of the experiments.

\para{Order and contrast}
Everything was run at fourth order, FD4 in one dimension and degree four
away from and degree three across an edge in two, at one material
contrast in each dimension (Section~\ref{sec:setting}) and one pulse per
test problem. Nothing was tried at a sharper contrast, for a layer
thinner than the $19\delta$ tails of an edge, or at an order above four
(Section~\ref{sec:results1d}). The construction does not change for any
of them, and the conditioning of Section~\ref{sec:chain}, the stability
at the standard $\gamma$ of Section~\ref{sec:stability2d} and the seeded
width were measured only where the tables say they were. In one
dimension an edge below about $10^{-5} h$ is better served by the
interface-aware weights directly, since the seeds converge to them at
first order in $\delta$ (Section~\ref{sec:assembly1d}).

\para{Node sets}
The largest node set has $19{,}600$ nodes, and the fine end of every
two-dimensional sweep is a resolution effect of the seeded region, not
of the edge: with no tolerance on the material spread the seeded rows
cover the $19\delta$ tails on both sides of both edges, $44$ to $72\%$
of the nodes, with $19$-node degree-three rows whose first derivatives
are third order, so the seeds' rates fall to $2.4$ to $2.7$ at the fine
end and the seed floor's own rate to $2.0$ between $10{,}000$ and
$19{,}600$ nodes at $\delta = 0.005$ (Section~\ref{sec:curvedresults}).
The trim to $3.8\delta$ restores rates of $3.2$ to $3.6$ without
lowering the error on these node sets. Where between $3.8\delta$ and
$19\delta$ to put the boundary of the seeded region beyond $19{,}600$
nodes was not settled, and no result here says what the seeds do on a
node set on which the trim would matter.

\para{The rule's evidence}
The rule of Section~\ref{sec:flatsweep} was set on $v$ at normal
incidence through flat edges and read off the same column on the curved
geometry (Section~\ref{sec:curvedresults}). At oblique incidence it was
checked on the seeded side only: the train does not reach below
$h = 0.71\delta$, where the seeds still lead by $1.6\times$
(Section~\ref{sec:oblique2d}), so nothing at oblique incidence says
where the seeds stop paying. The mode-converted S waves put every
scheme, the jump stencils included, at between second and
two-and-a-half order in the velocities at $26.6^\circ$ on these node
sets and do not register at $7^\circ$; fourth-order
behaviour at oblique incidence on this problem needs $h$ below about
$1 / 300$, the next node set up. The energy growth of the seed operator,
about $0.012$ per unit time at $\delta = 2h$
(Section~\ref{sec:stability2d}), was found only in the regime the rule
excludes, in which every row is a seed row, and was not chased.

\para{Geometry}
Route (a) evaluates seeds marched along one normal in the tangent frame
of the stencil, so the material at a stencil's far nodes is wrong by
$\kappa r^2 / 2$ against $\delta$ (Section~\ref{sec:curved2d}). On the
two sine interfaces run, at amplitude $0.02$, that error first shows
through $\delta = 0.0025$ at $10{,}000$ and $19{,}600$ nodes, as $1.4$
to $1.5\times$ the flat seeds' error, level with the seed floor, and
would lead at the next node set (Section~\ref{sec:curvedresults}). A
radius of curvature comparable to the stencil was not run, nor was the
oblique train on the curved geometry.

\para{Cost}
Each seed row is one or two marches of the $600$-state system of
Section~\ref{sec:elasticseeds}: $21$ to $25$\,ms for a 19-node stencil
and $53$\,ms for a seed row on a flat edge, $3.6\times$ that on a
curved one, and the two largest curved operators took $330$ and
$475$\,s on six workers (Sections~\ref{sec:assembly2d}
and~\ref{sec:curvedresults}). Neither the flat translation symmetry,
one march per row with dense output, nor the 19-node stencil as the
prefix of the 30-node one was used, and nothing is tuned; the operators
are cached, and the references of the curved geometry cost more than
everything else together ($45$ minutes plus $3.6$ hours). These are the
costs of the reference implementation and an upper bound on the
construction's.

\para{The comparison}
The coefficient treatments of Sections~\ref{sec:results1d}
and~\ref{sec:results2d} are our implementations, one scheme per
dimension, one contrast and one pulse, and their sources analyse and
run them on staggered Cartesian grids. The one-dimensional grid is
cell-centred with every jump on a cell boundary, which is why the
one-cell mean changes nothing at a jump there; the two-dimensional
averaging is an isotropic reading of the compliance rule, the wave
moduli harmonically and the density arithmetically over a square; and
the anisotropic effective medium of Schoenberg and Muir, which
\cite{KoeneWittstenRobertsson2022} find best in elastic media, was not
built because the implementation has no anisotropic five-field
operator. The measurement licenses what Section~\ref{sec:priorwork}
states and no more; it is not a ranking of the methods in general.

\subsection{Future work}
\label{sec:futurework}
In the order we would do them.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Curvilinear evaluation of the marched seeds}, before route
  (b) of Section~\ref{sec:curved2d}: keep the marches, evaluate each
  seed at the node's true arclength and normal distance instead of its
  tangent coordinates, and scale the tangential jets at the anchor by
  $1 / (1 - \kappa y_e)$. The material is then exact at every node at
  no march cost and only the operator's curvature terms remain. It is
  worth building where route (a)'s geometric floor, $\kappa r^2 / 2$
  against $\delta$, meets the resolution floor: the next node set up
  through $\delta = 0.0025$, or a tighter curve.
\item \emph{The seeded width.} The trim of
  Section~\ref{sec:curvedresults} is the lever that restores the order
  beyond $19{,}600$ nodes (extrapolating the two rates, the trimmed
  seeds lead by $40\%$ at $40{,}000$ nodes); where between $3.8\delta$
  and $19\delta$ to put the boundary of the seeded region is one sweep
  at the next node set.
\item \emph{The seeds' true $\Delta^3$ as hyperviscosity right-hand
  sides.} The hyperviscosity rows of Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d}
  impose $\Delta^3 S = 0$ on every seed, a choice; the alternative is
  the seeds' actual sixth derivatives at the anchor, which need the
  normal derivatives of the coefficient functions to sixth order and so
  the material's to fifth. Finite differences of the marched seeds
  cannot give them at a sharp edge (a sixth difference at step
  $\delta / 10$ amplifies rounding by $10^{13}$); a Taylor recursion on
  the first-order system at the anchor, with the Taylor coefficients of
  the coefficient matrix from the $\tanh$ recurrence
  $T' = (1 - T^2) / \delta$, can, in about sixty lines plus a test
  against the monomials' $\Delta^3$ in constant material. What it would
  do is predictable: at an anchor a distance $d$ from an edge the sixth
  derivative scales like $(r_{\max} / \delta)^5 e^{-2d / \delta}$, so
  the hyperviscosity would act on the seed part of the solution with
  strength $\gamma \Delta^3 S \propto (h / \delta)^5$, a consistent
  discretisation of $\gamma \Delta^3$ applied to the edge structure,
  strong exactly when the edge is unresolved. That is the behaviour the
  seeds exist to remove, so we expect a stable operator with more
  damping, not less, that damps the resolved physics at the edge, and
  would build it only if the spectrum or the errors ever called for it.
\item \emph{The cost of the marches.} The two optimisations Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d}
  names, one march per row on a flat edge and one march per stencil
  for both the 19- and the 30-node rows; and in one dimension
  M\"uhlbach's recurrence for generalised divided differences
  \cite{Muhlbach1973,Gerolymos2012}, the analogue of Fornberg's algorithm for an
  extended complete Chebyshev system, in place of the dense $5 \times 5$
  solve. None was needed for the sweeps.
\item \emph{Two runs the drivers can take today.} The oblique train on
  the curved geometry, since the product-grid reference of
  Section~\ref{sec:tests2d} takes any initial state, to see whether the
  converted-S floor of Section~\ref{sec:oblique2d} moves with
  curvature; and a dispersion study in a uniform medium of the
  $30$-node annihilating $\Delta^3$ rows, to settle why the band of
  seed rows is more accurate on this pulse than $30$-node degree-four
  stencils everywhere (Section~\ref{sec:flatsweep}), on which nothing
  here depends.
\item \emph{What the construction does not change for.} Higher order,
  which in two dimensions meets the instability
  Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d} found for degree-four rows across a
  sharp edge; other contrasts; a layer thinner than the $19\delta$ tails of
  its edges, where the two profiles overlap and the band never reaches
  its full contrast (Section~\ref{sec:setting2d}); the march itself
  passes through both edges of a band (Section~\ref{sec:assembly2d}),
  and it is only the jump path, at $\delta = 0$, that has no triple
  stencils for a band thinner than a stencil (Section~\ref{sec:scope}); the
  variable Lam\'e parameters of the published test case, which vary
  along the edge as well as across it, so that the ansatz of
  Section~\ref{sec:design2d}, the material a function of the normal
  coordinate alone, no longer applies as it stands; and three
  dimensions, where the ansatz has two tangential coordinates and the
  same normal one. The implementation is two-dimensional and none of
  these was run.
\item \emph{From the comparison.} An anisotropic five-field operator,
  which would let the Schoenberg--Muir medium that
  \cite{KoeneWittstenRobertsson2022} find best in elastic media run
  head to head with the seeds on the same nodes; and the seeds on a
  smoothed medium: applied to the medium the coefficient treatments
  manufacture, a seeded scheme would discretise that medium to fourth
  order and so separate the treatments' modelling error, the
  second-order component they accept at the interface, from their
  discretisation error, which is a hypothesis and nothing here tested.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
Between a medium that varies on the scale of the grid and one with a
jump lies the edge that is smooth but thinner than the grid, and the
question was whether a stencil on an unchanged grid or node set can
keep its order through it. On the node sets tested it can. The seeds
are the $t = 0$ profiles of polynomial-in-time solutions, continued
through the edge by ordinary differential equations rather than by
Taylor expansion, and they reduce to the interface-aware stencils of a
jump as $\delta \to 0$: one construction covers the jump, the sub-grid
edge and the resolved edge. In one dimension the seed stencils are
fourth order at every resolution with the errors of the jump case while
FD4 has its knee at $h = \delta$. In two dimensions the seeded RBF-FD
operator keeps the standard scheme's stability at the standard
hyperviscosity at every edge width, by the spectral and energy
measurements of Section~\ref{sec:stability2d},
and through an edge the nodes never resolve the seeds sit at the seed
operator's own floor, $20\times$ below the standard scheme in the
velocity and $67\times$ in the spurious tangential velocity at
$19{,}600$ nodes through a flat edge, and $3$ to $12\times$ below it
through a curved one. To our knowledge no earlier scheme builds a
stencil basis for the time-domain wave equation through a sub-grid
smooth edge by an ODE march while leaving the rest of the scheme
standard; the ingredients (bases from local solutions, polynomial
Trefftz spaces, the harmonic mean) are each classical. On the node sets
tested the rule is: seed when $\delta \le h$.

The comparison is against coefficient sampling on the same nodes and
against our implementation of the coefficient treatments of seismic
finite differences (cell means over one and two cells, band-limited
coefficients, a widened edge) on the same schemes, one contrast and one
test problem; their sources analyse and run them on staggered grids. On
these grids and node sets no coefficient treatment reaches the seeds'
order; the best of them is second order at a jump in one dimension, and
the seeds lie $3$ to $730$ times below it there and $3$ to $6$ times
below it on the scattered nodes.
